\begin{abstract}
Disagreement about what ``I love you'' means can become disagreement about what one person owes another. A later loss of love has different implications if the earlier declaration reported a feeling, made an undertaking, or gave words to an affective impulse. We investigate these possibilities as three nonexclusive dimensions: truth-presenting avowal (T), relational undertaking (P), and expressive/reflexive production (E). An automated analysis covers 225 retained occurrences extracted from 30 complete novels and plays in seven languages. The recorded judgments combine widespread T with variable P and E. Close readings provide textual grounds for distinguishing affection from undertaking and deliberate avowal from words represented as escaping inhibition. The contribution is an empirical basis for evaluating these first-level distinctions, supported by an inspectable and revisable annotation procedure. Finer questions remain about the loving relation, the precise illocutionary force and the participants' competing understandings. Establishing useful broad distinctions would nevertheless clarify why apparently identical declarations can support different expectations and accusations of betrayal.
\end{abstract}

\section{The disputed obligation}

Someone says ``I love you'' and later says, ``That was then. Now I don't love you any more.'' Has a promise been broken, or have feelings changed? Both parties might agree that the first declaration was sincere and still disagree about this. One understands it as having established an obligation that survives changes of feeling; the other understands it as having described how things stood at the time. Their disagreement concerns what the declaration did, as well as whether it was true.

A third possibility complicates the exchange. The speaker might say, ``I'm sorry. It just came out.'' On this account, the words were an expression produced by an emotional state before they could be considered. The appeal is to the circumstances of speaking rather than to a consciously adopted undertaking. Whether that explanation is credible, and what it excuses, are further questions. The distinction matters because a hearer may have understood the same words as a commitment and made consequential decisions in reliance on them.

These are constructed possibilities, but the problem they identify is substantive. Disagreement about a love declaration can affect expectations of fidelity, care, marriage or a shared future. It can also change the grounds for blame. If an undertaking was made, we must ask what was promised and whether it was honoured. If a feeling was avowed, we must consider sincerity and whatever responsibility attaches to changing feelings. If words emerged as an affective response, deliberation and control become relevant. None of these descriptions settles the ethical judgment by itself. They identify different questions that such a judgment must answer.

The familiar complaint ``I don't know what love means'' can thus express uncertainty about the consequences of saying the words, as well as uncertainty about the feeling itself. A fixed, universally shared rule connecting declarations to obligations would simplify the problem. Our hypothesis is that actual uses admit more than one interpretation. If speakers encounter the formula as an avowal, a pledge and an emotional outburst, learning from those examples need not give them an agreed rule for a new occasion. This is a possible source of misunderstanding, including among competent speakers. We do not need to assume that every disagreement results from an immature understanding of language: intention, reasonable uptake and subsequent reinterpretation may diverge.

The hypothesis is easy to illustrate and harder to establish. Invented examples may build the desired interpretation into their descriptions. Familiar literary quotations may depend on omitted context, and memorable cases may be unrepresentative. Perhaps one interpretation dominates almost completely; perhaps apparently different functions almost always occur together. A collection of plausible examples leaves these alternatives unresolved.

We address that evidential problem through a systematic analysis of a defined multilingual literary corpus. Explicit extraction and inclusion rules yield 225 retained occurrences from 30 complete novels and plays in seven languages. An AI annotator assesses support for three candidate interpretations---truth-presenting avowal (T), relational undertaking (P), and expressive/reflexive production (E)---using supplied context and recording reasons and evidence. We examine both the distribution of those judgments and the passages on which important contrasts depend. The study's contribution is to put consequential interpretive distinctions on a firmer, publicly examinable footing. An automated procedure also makes it practical to apply revised criteria across the same material when a criticism warrants doing so.

T/P/E is proposed as a first level of analysis. Establishing that these dimensions distinguish uses would be progress even without a complete account of love, a taxonomy of every speech act, or a verdict on the conduct of every speaker. Our immediate evidence concerns literary representations of love language. These representations offer a way to investigate the distinctions and their stakes; their frequencies cannot be assumed to reproduce ordinary conversation.

\section{Three dimensions, with further distinctions within them}

We use ILY as a convenient label for the expressions under investigation. Three deliberately constructed examples introduce the dimensions; they are illustrations, not additional corpus observations.

\begin{description}[style=nextline]
\item[T: truth-presenting avowal.]
Asked whether a feeling remains, someone answers: ``Yes, I love you. But I am not promising that we have a future together.'' The speaker presents a loving state as true while withholding an undertaking. T identifies this avowing or reporting function; it does not certify sincerity. A lie can have the same form.
\item[P: relational undertaking.]
A couple renewing their commitment use ``I love you'' as a pledge to care for one another, and both understand the exchange that way. The expression participates in an undertaking. The operational category also covers enacting, renewing or invoking a commitment or obligation; affection, reassurance or a reference to a shared future does not by itself establish P.
\item[E: expressive/reflexive production.]
Overcome by affection, someone hears herself say ``I love you!'' before she has decided to speak. Here the emotional state is represented as producing the words before deliberation. An exclamation mark, tears or intense delivery alone would not establish E under the annotation criteria.
\end{description}

The dimensions are nonexclusive. A sincere avowal can also renew a commitment; an affective outburst can avow love and participate in an undertaking. An E interpretation therefore does not automatically defeat P, and a T interpretation does not rule it out. The central question for our motivating problem is when an undertaking is supported in addition to, or independently of, evidence about the feeling and its expression.

The framework deliberately leaves questions open within each dimension. Under T, what loving relation is presented: romantic attachment, familial affection, friendship, or some other relation? Under P, what is the precise illocutionary force: a promise, a renewal, an acceptance of an undertaking, or an invocation of an existing obligation? What does it concern, and under what conditions? Under E, how should expressive discharge be distinguished from reflex-like or unplanned verbal production? These further distinctions can matter greatly. They do not make the broader questions dispensable. Before specifying the content of a promise, we need evidence that an undertaking is involved at all.

The dimensions also concern different aspects of an occurrence: what is presented, what is undertaken, and how expression is produced. We do not assume that each corresponds to a separate dictionary sense. Nor does P encompass every action performed in speaking. Avowing is itself an act; P has the narrower meaning specified above. This restriction is compatible with Austin's attention to what utterances accomplish in appropriate circumstances \citep{austin1975}.

Barthes provides a provocative point of comparison. In the ``I Love You'' fragment of \emph{A Lover's Discourse}, he describes the formula as having ``no other referent than its utterance: it is a performative'' \citep[p.~148]{barthes1978}. His declared focus is the repeated love cry after the first avowal \citep[p.~147]{barthes1978}. That restriction, and the difference between his performativity and our P, preclude treating every T/P contrast below as a direct refutation. His account nevertheless sharpens the question of how a distinctive treatment of the formula relates to the variety of its contextual uses.

An additional annotation category, O, identifies an important aspect of the core expression inadequately represented by any combination of T/P/E. It provides a way to register pressure against the framework. The present inquiry concerns the evidence for distinctions and combinations; it does not presuppose that the three dimensions exhaust the phenomena.

\section{A corpus and a procedure open to criticism}

The processed public corpus comprises eight English works, ten French works, four Norwegian works, and two each in Danish, German, Italian and Swedish. Extraction patterns were applied across the complete canonical texts. They identified 227 candidates; automated membership review retained 225, all subsequently annotated under prompt version 0.3.1. A thirty-first, private work in the wider project is outside this snapshot. The membership record remains provisional pending human review \citep{lmcw2026}.

Searching complete works gives routine occurrences a place alongside memorable ones and fixes the scope within which the judgments are counted. It does not make the selection of works random or guarantee recall of every possible love expression. The rules include negation, tense and modal variants, embedding, and represented or hypothetical speech. We therefore call the units \emph{ILY-occurrences}: not every match is a declaration actually made within the story. Corresponding lexical constructions in seven languages provide comparative material without establishing complete semantic equivalence or representative coverage of cultures.

Literary texts supply a common, inspectable object of interpretation. They may represent intentions, responses and prior exchanges that an isolated quotation conceals. Shared access to that wording does not require agreement about its meaning. Narration counts here as evidence about the represented event, with attention to whose account it is; it is not observational access to the mental states of real speakers.

The saved requests use GPT-5.6 Sol and provide local text and wider context, approximately 3,000 characters on either side expanded to paragraph boundaries. Non-English requests include a generated translation as an aid; the original-language wording remains authoritative. The prompt permits reliable background knowledge of the work but requires its use to be identified separately. Outputs contain independent integer support scores from 0 to 4, an event-status label, reasons, evidence and uncertainty. The scores are not probabilities and need not sum to a fixed total. T does not measure sincerity, P does not measure eventual fidelity, and E does not measure emotional intensity.

The prompt imposes a deliberately high burden of proof on O and a restrictive interpretation of E. Low O cannot therefore independently establish the adequacy of T/P/E, and low E cannot establish that love declarations are seldom emotional. The numerical results describe decisions made under these operational criteria. Their interpretive value depends on how well the cited passages warrant them.

The methodological advantage is that the criteria, supplied contexts and outputs remain available for criticism. A reader can challenge a distinction or the treatment of context, formulate a revised protocol, and apply it to the same corpus. A human team could also publish and revise an annotation protocol; automation changes the labour required for repeated application across languages. The frozen run records an estimated API cost of US\$14.50 for 225 classifications, excluding corpus preparation and method development. This is a recorded estimate, not a complete costing of the research. Model judgments may vary on repetition, and publication of a prompt and explanation does not expose every influence on an answer. Comparisons under revised criteria remain future work.

For exploratory close reading, 28 annotation records from 15 works were examined purposively, covering all seven languages. Selection included ordinary avowals, strong P and E scores, lower T scores, the positive O case, and different kinds of represented speech. Central comparisons were checked against their saved source passages. This reading is an interpretation of the records, not an independent reliability experiment. The accompanying notes preserve the selection register and immutable links. No historical score has been altered.

At this threshold, every occurrence supporting P or E also supports T. The four records below threshold on all three dimensions are hypothetical occurrences with T1.

These counts locate contrasts beyond a single selected passage, but their force must be kept distinct from that of close reading. High T partly reflects extraction of explicit love constructions and the avowal criterion. P and E depend on the specified instructions. Several occurrences share a passage and its evidence, so the totals are not counts of independent scenes or speakers. Repetition across works makes the candidate distinctions worth examining; it does not by itself validate the annotator's interpretation.

\section{What the passages distinguish}

\subsection{Avowing love while disputing an obligation}

In \emph{Jane Eyre}, St John accuses Jane of retreating from a promise to join missionary work. Jane, who also narrates the encounter, insists that she has entered no such engagement. Her reply includes ``no dishonour, no breach of promise, no desertion'' and then ``as a sister, I love you'' \citep[chap.~XXXV]{bronteJaneEyre}. The passage itself connects avowal with a dispute about obligation. The recorded T4/P0/E0 interpretation is supported by Jane's assertion of affection alongside her explicit rejection of the undertaking attributed to her.

The phrase ``as a sister'' also shows what T alone leaves unspecified. Jane identifies a loving relation while resisting the marital and missionary role St John demands. Classifying the avowal is useful, but understanding its content requires this further distinction. Her assertion does not make her socially unconnected or incapable of obligations; it does not undertake the particular obligations contested here.

The dispute in \emph{Middlemarch} concerns romantic expectations directly. Mary tells Fred, ``If I did love you, I would not marry you.'' Fred replies that if she loves him she ought to promise marriage; she contests that inference \citep[chap.~XIV]{eliotMiddlemarch}. Her conditional does not itself avow love, which helps explain its T1/P0/E0 annotation. It nevertheless gives unusually explicit textual evidence for the conceptual distinction: the characters argue over whether affection requires a marital undertaking. The dispute is present in their exchange, independently of our labels.

Nora's ``Jeg elsker deg ikke mer''---``I do not love you any more''---near the end of \emph{Et dukkehjem} receives T4/P0/E0 \citep[act~III]{ibsenDukkehjem}. On that reading, she avows a changed condition while articulating departure elsewhere in the exchange. This does not settle whether she has fulfilled every obligation of the marriage. It separates the function of these words from a moral verdict on her departure. A reader who finds the declaration itself enact a rupture must explain whether that is a relational undertaking in our operational sense. The quotation follows the modernized Runeberg text used in the corpus.

\subsection{Avowal participating in an undertaking}

In Nansen's \emph{Maria}, the narrator answers a question about the durability of his love with ``Jeg elsker Dig i Lyst og i N\o d''---``I love you in joy and in adversity''---and develops this into the prospect of a shared lifetime \citep[sec.~LXI]{nansenMaria}. The T4/P4/E0 reading treats the avowal as participating in a vow-like assurance. Support for P comes from the question, the formula and the developed undertaking together. The present-tense love clause does more here than locate a feeling at the moment of speaking.

Wentworth's letter in \emph{Persuasion} provides a less unequivocal combination. ``I have loved none but you'' claims constancy, while ``I offer myself to you again'' makes the renewed offer explicit \citep[chap.~XXIII]{austenPersuasion}. The love occurrence receives T4/P2/E0. The intermediate P score leaves room to distinguish the avowal's contribution to the offer from an undertaking accomplished by that clause itself. Promise, offer and renewal are finer questions than the presence of a relational undertaking.

Together these passages identify positive and negative grounds for P: an explicit denial of the alleged engagement, a dispute over whether love entails marriage, and an avowal embedded in an assurance of continuing union. This is the first-level contrast the study seeks to substantiate. It survives reasonable disagreement about the exact scores; the claim that P2 and P4 constitute a consistently measurable difference would require further calibration.

\subsection{A feeling represented as producing the words}

In Ania Cofield's \emph{Nikki's Touch}, Nikki's declaration is narrated as words ``spilling out before she could stop them'' \citep{cofieldNikkisTouch}. The T4/P0/E4 annotation has explicit support for production preceding inhibition. The following narration presents the admission as a release and as honest. E here accompanies T: the proposed distinction concerns how the avowal is produced, without cancelling its presentation of a loving state. Wentworth's emotional difficulty in writing, by contrast, supplies no comparably direct evidence that the love words escape his control.

Nothing in the E label alone tells us how Nikki's interlocutor ought to interpret the declaration, or what she would owe him later. Those questions require attention to uptake and subsequent conduct. The narrative evidence nevertheless supports distinguishing this production from a considered assertion or undertaking. That distinction is relevant to the sort of retrospective explanation imagined in our opening, without automatically vindicating such an explanation.

Flaubert provides a less determinate case. Two occurrences in Emma's recurrent declarations to Rodolphe receive E3, with the annotation invoking the narrator's image of feeling overflowing through conventional metaphors \citep{flaubertBovary}. Emotional overflow supports an expressive reading but establishes involuntary verbal production less directly than Nikki's passage. Rodolphe's dismissal of conventional words and the narrator's challenge to that dismissal also make the source of an interpretation consequential. E therefore gathers a further distinction that closer analysis should preserve.

\section{The limits clarify the next questions}

Some difficulties concern the object being scored. One \emph{Jane Eyre} record receives T4/P0/E3 because St John's bodily response betrays attraction to Rosamond. He does not utter the represented declaration: Jane renders his look as an imagined speech \citep[chap.~XXXII]{bronteJaneEyre}. The saved status correctly identifies a nonverbal event verbalized in narration. Involuntary bodily expression, however, cannot count as direct evidence of reflexive verbal production. This case limits the use of aggregate E counts while clarifying what evidence an E claim needs.

Similarly, Mary's conditional withholds an assertion; it does not introduce a new kind of loving relation. An imagined declaration by Jane to Rochester receives T3/P4 because the contemplated words would undertake a shared life. Comparing these cases requires distinguishing an enacted avowal, embedded content and the force of a possible utterance. The status labels assist that comparison but do not fully resolve it. The direct-speech column in Table~\ref{tab:patterns} provides one restricted view, not a complete correction for these differences.

Lawrence supplies a different difficulty. Two nearby occurrences in \emph{Women in Love} receive T4/P3/E0/O0 and T3/P1/E0/O3; the latter accompanies reflection on the inadequacy of the conventional formula \citep[chap.~XXVII]{lawrenceWomenInLove}. Related contextual material is thus compatible with the framework in one record and pressure against it in another. Since the targets differ, this is not by itself a contradiction. It is a specific demand for justification: what belongs to the function of each target, and what belongs to commentary around it? The published records make that judgment available for reconsideration.

\section{Discussion: establishing a first level of analysis}

The strongest evidence for T/P/E comes from what the distinctions enable us to identify in the texts. Jane explicitly avows affection while denying an undertaking. Mary disputes an inference from romantic love to marriage. Nansen's love clause participates in an assurance about a continuing union. Nikki's words are represented as emerging before inhibition. These provide different kinds of warrant, and they bear on the problem of what a speaker has done by declaring love. The recorded distributions place these comparisons within a systematic collection and show how the same criteria have been applied elsewhere.

Establishing this first level would matter even if further analysis substantially refined it. T leaves the nature of the loving relation open; P leaves the precise act and its terms open; E leaves important differences in expressive production open. Such incompleteness is compatible with explanatory progress. Recognizing that an undertaking is disputed makes it possible to ask what its content would be. Recognizing avowal distinguishes that question from whether the feeling was present. Recognizing affective production identifies a different kind of evidence about the event of speaking.

The ethical significance consequently lies in the grounds for expectations and accountability. A sincere avowal is not automatically a promise of permanence, and an emotional outburst is not automatically free of commitments. Neither a hearer's expectation nor a speaker's later preferred description settles what occurred. A fuller interpretation must consider intention, uptake, circumstances and the kind of relationship at issue. Literary narration can make those perspectives visible, including their conflicts. The present scores summarize an analysis of each passage; they are not separate measurements of every participant's understanding.

There are two immediate ways to strengthen the evidence. Independent readings can test the warrants offered for particular cases. Comparisons using altered instructions or amounts of context can test how stable the aggregate patterns are. Context may support a low score as well as a high one: Jane's denial matters to P0 just as the description of speech escaping inhibition matters to E4. The existing annotations expose these questions; they do not substitute for carrying out the comparisons.

Our contribution is therefore a contextualized body of evidence for evaluating T/P/E, an initial account of its patterns, and a procedure through which objections can lead to systematic revision. The close readings support useful distinctions between avowal, undertaking and affective production, while revealing where the present operationalization needs refinement. That is a substantive first step toward explaining why the same familiar words can support sharply different understandings of what has been said and what is owed.
