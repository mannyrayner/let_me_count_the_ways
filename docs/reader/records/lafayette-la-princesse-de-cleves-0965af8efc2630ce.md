## English translation supplied to the annotator

> “I have loved you too much to let you believe that the change you perceive in me is an effect of my fickleness; I want you to know that your unfaithfulness is the cause of it. You are greatly surprised that I speak to you of your unfaithfulness; you had concealed it from me with such skill, and I took such care to conceal from you that I knew of it, that you have reason to be astonished that it is known to me. I am surprised myself that I was able to keep you from seeing any sign of it. Never has any sorrow equaled mine. I believed that you had a violent passion for me; I no longer concealed from you the passion I had for you, and at the very time when I was letting you see it in its entirety, I learned that you were deceiving me, that you loved another woman, and that, to all appearances, you were sacrificing me to this new mistress. I learned it on the day of the running at the ring; that is why I did not go. I pretended to be ill in order to conceal the disorder of my mind; but I truly became ill, and my body could not withstand so violent an agitation. When I began to recover, I still pretended to be very ill, so that I might have a pretext for neither seeing you nor writing to you. I wanted time to decide how I ought to behave toward you; twenty times I adopted and abandoned the same resolutions; but at last I found you unworthy of seeing my sorrow, and I resolved not to let it show. I wanted to wound your pride by making you see that my passion was weakening of its own accord. I believed that I would thereby diminish the value of the sacrifice you were making of it; I did not want you to have the pleasure of showing how much I loved you in order to appear more lovable on that account. I resolved to write you cold and languid letters, so as to put into the mind of the woman to whom you gave them the thought that you were ceasing to be loved. I did not want her to have the pleasure of learning that I knew she was triumphing over me, nor to increase her triumph by my despair and my reproaches. I thought that I would not punish you enough by breaking with you, and that I would cause you only slight pain if I ceased to love you when you no longer loved me. I concluded that you had to love me in order to feel the pain of not being loved, which I was experiencing so cruelly. I believed that if anything could rekindle the feelings you had once had for me, it was to make you see that mine had changed; but to make you see it while pretending to conceal it from you, and as though I had not had the strength to confess it to you. I settled upon this resolution; but how difficult it was for me to make, and, when I saw you again, how impossible it seemed to carry out! A hundred times I was on the verge of bursting forth in reproaches and tears; the state to which my health had still reduced me helped me disguise my distress and affliction from you. I was then sustained by the pleasure of dissembling with you, as you dissembled with me; nevertheless, I did myself such great violence in order to tell you and write to you that I loved you that you saw sooner than I had intended to let you see that my feelings had changed. You were hurt by it; you complained of it. I tried to reassure you; but it was in so forced a manner that you were still more thoroughly convinced that I no longer loved you. At last, I did everything I had intended to do. The capriciousness of your heart made you return to me as you saw me withdrawing from you. I have enjoyed all the pleasure that vengeance can give; it has seemed to me that you loved me more than you had ever done, and I have made you see that I no longer loved you. I have had reason to believe that you had entirely abandoned the woman for whom you had left me. I have also had reasons to be convinced that you had never spoken to her about me; but your return and your discretion have not been able to make amends for your fickleness. Your heart has been divided between me and another woman; you have deceived me. That is enough to deprive me of the pleasure of being loved by you as I believed I deserved to be, and to leave me in the resolution I have made never to see you again, at which you are so surprised.
> 
> Madame de Clèves read this letter and reread it several times, without, however, knowing what she had read. She saw only that Monsieur de Nemours did not love her as she had thought, and that he loved other women whom he deceived as he deceived her. What a sight and what knowledge for a person of her disposition, who had a violent passion, who had just given signs of it to a man she judged unworthy of it, and to another man whom she treated badly for his sake! Never had affliction been so piercing and so acute: it seemed to her that what made this affliction so bitter was what had happened that day, and that, if Monsieur de Nemours had had no reason to believe that she loved him, she would not have cared that he had loved another woman. But she was deceiving herself; and the pain she found so unbearable was jealousy, with all the horrors that may accompany it. She saw from this letter that Monsieur de Nemours had long been engaged in a love affair. She found that the woman who had written the letter had intelligence and merit; she seemed to her worthy of being loved; she found in her more courage than she found in herself, and she envied the strength she had shown in concealing her feelings from Monsieur de Nemours. She saw from the end of the letter that this person believed herself loved; she thought that the discretion this prince had shown her, and by which she had been so deeply moved, was perhaps only the effect of the passion he had for that other person, whom he feared to displease. In short, she thought of everything that could increase her affliction and her despair. How she turned her thoughts back upon herself! What reflections she made upon the advice her mother had given her! How she repented of not having persisted in withdrawing from society, despite Monsieur de Clèves, or of not having followed the thought she had had of confessing to him the inclination she had for Monsieur de Nemours! She found that she would have done better to reveal it to a husband whose goodness she knew, and who would have had an interest in concealing it, than to let it be seen by a man who was unworthy of it, who deceived her, who perhaps sacrificed her, and who desired to be loved by her only out of pride and vanity. In short, she found that all the misfortunes that could befall her, and all the extremes to which she might be driven, were less dreadful than having let Monsieur de Nemours see that she loved him and knowing that he loved another woman. All that consoled her was the thought that, after acquiring this knowledge, she at least had nothing more to fear from herself, and that she would be entirely cured of the inclination she had for this prince.”
