It was impossible for him to remain with the Queen; he returned home, not even knowing why he was returning or whether he intended to go and interrupt Monsieur de Nemours. As soon as he drew near his house, he looked to see whether he could discern anything that might enable him to judge whether that prince was still there: he felt relief on seeing that he was no longer there, and found comfort in thinking that he could not have remained there long. He imagined that perhaps it was not Monsieur de Nemours of whom he ought to be jealous; and although he had no doubt of it, he sought to doubt it; but so many things would have convinced him of it that he did not remain long in the uncertainty he desired. He went at once to his wife’s chamber, and after speaking to her for some time about indifferent matters, he could not keep himself from asking her what she had done and whom she had seen; she gave him an account of it. When he saw that she did not name Monsieur de Nemours, he asked her, trembling, whether that was everyone she had seen, in order to give her an opportunity to name that prince and to avoid the pain of having her dissemble the matter with him. Since she had not seen him, she did not name him; and Monsieur de Clèves resumed, in a tone that revealed his distress:

But, Madame, he has spoken to you; if his silence alone had revealed his passion to you, it would not have made so great an impression upon you. You could not tell me the whole truth; you concealed the greater part of it from me; you even repented of the little you confessed to me, and you did not have the strength to continue. I am more unhappy than I believed, and I am the unhappiest of all men. You are my wife, I love you as my mistress, and I see you love another.
