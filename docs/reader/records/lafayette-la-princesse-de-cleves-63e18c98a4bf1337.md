## Original AI explanation

Monsieur de Clèves directly presents his former love for his wife as a true, exceptionally deep emotional state. The imperfect « je vous aimais » is retrospective but still plainly avowal-like and truth-conditional. In this comparative clause he does not undertake or renew a relational commitment; instead, he contrasts his past marital love with the allegedly self-interested professions of seducers. Although the larger speech is emotionally uncontrolled and delivered near death, the highlighted words function as part of a reasoned accusation and self-description rather than as a reflex-like affective discharge.

## Narrative situation (AI interpretation)

Dying and believing himself cruelly deceived, Monsieur de Clèves uses the avowal within a farewell, reproach, and moral contrast. He characterizes his own passion as true and legitimate and opposes it to men who profess love merely to gain the honor of seduction. This framing makes the utterance accusatory and self-vindicating, but those functions operate through a claim about the reality and quality of his past love rather than through a new undertaking.

She never left her husband’s room and was in violent grief over the condition in which she saw him. Monsieur de Nemours was in despair that she was so afflicted; he easily judged how greatly this affliction renewed the affection she had for Monsieur de Clèves, and how greatly this affection dangerously diverted her from the passion she had in her heart. This thought caused him mortal anguish for some time; but the extremity of Monsieur de Clèves’s illness opened new hopes to him. He saw that Madame de Clèves might perhaps be free to follow her inclination, and that he might find in the future a succession of lasting happiness and pleasures. He could not endure this thought, so much agitation and rapture did it cause him, and he turned his mind away from it for fear of finding himself too unhappy if he should lose his hopes.
> 
> Meanwhile, Monsieur de Clèves had been almost given up by the physicians. On one of the last days of his illness, after spending a very distressing night, he said toward morning that he wished to rest. Madame de Clèves remained alone in his room; it seemed to her that, instead of resting, he was very uneasy. She approached and came to kneel before his bed, her face entirely covered with tears. Monsieur de Clèves had resolved not to show her the violent resentment he felt against her; but the care she lavished on him and her affliction, which sometimes seemed genuine to him and which he also sometimes regarded as signs of dissimulation and perfidy, caused such conflicting and painful feelings in him that he could not contain them within himself.
> 
> —You shed many tears, Madame, he said to her, for a death that you are causing and that cannot give you the grief you display. I am no longer in a condition to reproach you, he continued, in a voice weakened by illness and sorrow; but I am dying of the cruel pain you have caused me. Was it necessary that an action as extraordinary as your speaking to me at Coulommiers should have so little consequence? Why enlighten me about the passion you had for Monsieur de Nemours if your virtue did not extend far enough to resist it? I loved you to the point of being glad to be deceived, I confess it to my shame; I have regretted that false peace from which you roused me. Why did you not leave me in that tranquil blindness enjoyed by so many husbands? I might have remained ignorant all my life that you loved Monsieur de Nemours. I shall die, he added; but know that you make death welcome to me, and that, after depriving me of the esteem and tenderness I had for you, life would fill me with horror. What would I do with life, he went on, whether I spent it with a person whom I loved so greatly and by whom I have been so cruelly deceived, or lived apart from that same person and went so far as a public scandal and acts of violence so contrary to my disposition and to the passion I had for you? It went beyond what you saw of it, Madame; I concealed the greater part of it from you for fear of importuning you or of losing some of your esteem through conduct unbecoming to a husband. In short, I deserved your heart; once again, I die without regret, since I could not have it and can no longer desire it. Farewell, Madame; one day you will regret a man who loved you with a true and legitimate passion. You will feel the sorrow that reasonable people find in such attachments, and you will know the difference between being loved as I loved you and being loved by people who, while professing love for you, seek only the honor of seducing you. But my death will leave you free, he added, and you will be able to make Monsieur de Nemours happy without its costing you the commission of crimes. What does it matter, he went on, what will happen when I am no more, and must I be so weak as to turn my eyes toward it!
> 
> Madame de Clèves was so far from imagining that her husband could harbor suspicions against her that she listened to all these words without understanding them and with no other idea than that he was reproaching her for her inclination toward Monsieur de Nemours; at last, suddenly emerging from her blindness:

> —Say no more, Monsieur de Clèves interrupted; false oaths or a confession might cause me equal pain.
