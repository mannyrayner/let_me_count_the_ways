> She had withdrawn entirely from society to devote herself to her feelings for Oswald. But at last, wounded by his silence about their future, she resolved to accept an invitation to a ball where her presence was eagerly desired. Nothing is regarded with more indifference in Rome than withdrawing from society and reappearing in it by turns, as it suits one: it is the country where people concern themselves least with what elsewhere is called gossip; everyone does as he pleases without anyone inquiring into it, unless in others one encounters an obstacle to one's love or ambition. The Romans are no more concerned about the conduct of their compatriots than about that of the foreigners who come and go through their city, the meeting place of Europeans. When Lord Nelvil learned that Corinne was going to the ball, he felt vexed. For some time he had thought he saw in her a melancholy disposition that sympathized with his own; all at once she appeared to him keenly preoccupied with dancing, with that talent in which she excelled, and her imagination seemed animated by the prospect of a celebration. Corinne was not a frivolous person, but every day she felt herself more subjugated by her love for Oswald, and she wanted to try to weaken its force. She knew from experience that reflection and sacrifices have less power over passionate natures than distraction does, and she thought that reason consists not in triumphing over oneself according to the rules, but as best one can.
> 
> “I must,” she said to Lord Nelvil, who was reproaching her for this intention, “I must nevertheless find out whether there is no one but you left in the world who can fill my life, whether what pleased me formerly can no longer amuse me, and whether the feeling you inspire in me must absorb every other interest and every other thought.”—“So you wish to cease loving me?” Oswald replied.—“No,” Corinne answered; “but only in domestic life can it be sweet to feel oneself thus ruled by a single affection. For me, who need my talents, my wit, my imagination, to sustain the brilliance of the life I have adopted, it hurts me, and hurts me greatly, to love as I love you.”—“So you would not sacrifice to me,” Oswald said to her, “that homage, that glory?”—“What does it matter to you,” Corinne said, “to know whether I would sacrifice them to you!
