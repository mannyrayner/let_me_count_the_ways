## English translation supplied to the annotator

> “I have loved you too much to let you believe that the change you perceive in me is an effect of my fickleness; I wish to let you know that your unfaithfulness is the cause of it. You are greatly surprised that I should speak to you of your unfaithfulness; you had concealed it from me with such skill, and I took such care to conceal from you that I knew of it, that you have good reason to be astonished that it is known to me. I am surprised myself that I could have kept you from seeing any sign of it. Never has any sorrow equaled mine. I believed that you had a violent passion for me; I no longer concealed from you the passion I had for you, and at the very time when I was letting you see it in its entirety, I learned that you were deceiving me, that you loved another woman, and that, to all appearances, you were sacrificing me to this new mistress. I learned it on the day of the ring-tilting contest; that is why I did not go. I pretended to be ill in order to conceal the turmoil in my mind; but I became ill in truth, and my body could not withstand so violent an agitation. When I began to recover, I again pretended to be very ill, so as to have a pretext for neither seeing you nor writing to you. I wished to have time to decide how I ought to deal with you; twenty times I adopted and abandoned the same resolutions; but at last I found you unworthy of seeing my sorrow, and I resolved not to let you perceive it. I wished to wound your pride by showing you that my passion was weakening of its own accord. I thought thereby to diminish the value of the sacrifice you were making of it; I did not wish you to have the pleasure of showing how much I loved you in order to appear more lovable. I resolved to write you cool and listless letters, so as to instill in the mind of the woman to whom you gave them the idea that you were ceasing to be loved. I did not wish her to have the pleasure of learning that I knew she was triumphing over me, or to increase her triumph through my despair and my reproaches. I thought that I would not punish you enough by breaking with you, and that I would cause you only slight pain if I ceased to love you when you no longer loved me. I concluded that you had to love me in order to feel the pain of not being loved, which I was experiencing so cruelly. I thought that if anything could rekindle the feelings you had had for me, it was to show you that mine had changed; but to show it to you while pretending to conceal it from you, as though I had not had the strength to confess it to you. I settled upon this resolution; but how difficult it was for me to make, and how impossible it seemed to me to carry out when I saw you again! A hundred times I was on the verge of bursting out with reproaches and tears; the state in which my health still left me helped me disguise my distress and affliction from you. I was then sustained by the pleasure of dissembling with you, as you dissembled with me; nevertheless, I did myself such great violence in telling you and writing to you that I loved you that you saw sooner than I had intended to let you see that my feelings had changed. You were wounded by it; you complained of it. I tried to reassure you; but it was in so forced a manner that you were all the more convinced that I no longer loved you. At last, I did everything I had intended to do. The capriciousness of your heart brought you back toward me as you saw me drawing away from you. I have enjoyed all the pleasure that vengeance can give; it seemed to me that you loved me more than you ever had, and I showed you that I no longer loved you. I had reason to believe that you had entirely abandoned the woman for whom you had left me. I also had reasons to be convinced that you had never spoken to her of me; but your return and your discretion have not been able to make amends for your fickleness. Your heart was divided between me and another; you deceived me; that is enough to deprive me of the pleasure of being loved by you as I believed I deserved to be, and to leave me in the resolution I have made never to see you again, at which you are so surprised.
> 
> Madame de Clèves read this letter and reread it several times, without, however, knowing what she had read. She saw only that Monsieur de Nemours did not love her as she had thought, and that he loved other women whom he deceived as he did her. What a thing to see and to know for a person of her disposition, who had a violent passion, who had just given signs of it to a man whom she judged unworthy of it, and to another whom she treated badly for his sake! Never had any affliction been so piercing and so acute: it seemed to her that what made this affliction so bitter was what had happened that day, and that, if Monsieur de Nemours had had no reason to believe that she loved him, she would not have cared that he had loved another woman. But she was deceiving herself; and the pain she found so unbearable was jealousy, with all the horrors that can accompany it. She saw from this letter that Monsieur de Nemours had long been involved in a love affair. She found that the woman who had written the letter had wit and merit; she seemed to her worthy of being loved; she found in her more courage than she found in herself, and she envied the strength she had had to conceal her feelings from Monsieur de Nemours. She saw from the end of the letter that this woman believed herself loved; she thought that the discretion this prince had shown her, and by which she had been so deeply touched, was perhaps only the effect of the passion he had for this other woman, whom he feared to displease. In short, she thought everything that could increase her affliction and her despair. How she turned her thoughts back upon herself! What reflections she made upon the advice her mother had given her! How she regretted not having persisted in withdrawing from society, despite Monsieur de Clèves, or not having followed through on her thought of confessing to him the inclination she had for Monsieur de Nemours! She thought that she would have done better to reveal it to a husband whose goodness she knew, and who would have had an interest in concealing it, than to let it be seen by a man who was unworthy of it, who deceived her, who was perhaps sacrificing her, and who sought to be loved by her only out of pride and vanity. At last, she concluded that all the misfortunes that could befall her, and all the extremes to which she might be driven, were less than having let Monsieur de Nemours see that she loved him and knowing that he loved another woman. All that consoled her was the thought, at least, that after gaining this knowledge she had nothing more to fear from herself, and that she would be entirely cured of the inclination she had for this prince.”
