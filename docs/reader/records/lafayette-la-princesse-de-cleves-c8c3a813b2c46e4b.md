> The ladies de Nevers and de Martigues, on leaving her house, went to the Queen-Dauphiness’s apartments; Monsieur de Clèves was there. That princess asked them where they had come from; they told her that they had come from Monsieur de Clèves’s house, where they had spent part of the afternoon with a great many people, and that they had left no one there but Monsieur de Nemours. These words, which they thought so inconsequential, were not so to Monsieur de Clèves. Although he must well have imagined that Monsieur de Nemours might often find opportunities to speak to his wife, nevertheless the thought that he was at her house, that he was there alone, and that he might speak to her of his love, seemed to him at that moment so novel and so unbearable that jealousy flared up in his heart more violently than it ever had before. It was impossible for him to remain with the Queen; he returned home, not even knowing why he was returning, or whether he intended to go and interrupt Monsieur de Nemours. As soon as he approached his house, he looked to see whether he might notice anything that would enable him to judge whether that prince was still there: he felt relieved when he saw that he was no longer there, and found comfort in thinking that he could not have remained long. He imagined that perhaps Monsieur de Nemours was not the man of whom he ought to be jealous; and although he did not doubt it at all, he sought to doubt it; but so many things would have convinced him of it that he did not remain long in the uncertainty he desired. He went at once to his wife’s chamber, and after speaking to her for some time about indifferent matters, he could not keep himself from asking her what she had done and whom she had seen; she gave him an account of it. When he saw that she did not name Monsieur de Nemours, he asked her, trembling, whether that was everyone she had seen, in order to give her an opportunity to name that prince and to spare himself the pain of her practicing a deception upon him concerning it. As she had not seen him, she did not name him; and Monsieur de Clèves, resuming in a tone that showed his distress, said to her:
> 
> “And Monsieur de Nemours—did you not see him, or have you forgotten him?”
> 
> “I did not see him, in fact,” she replied. “I was unwell, and I sent one of my women to make my apologies to him.”
> 
> “Then you were unwell only for him,” Monsieur de Clèves rejoined. “Since you saw everyone else, why make distinctions for Monsieur de Nemours? Why is he not like any other man to you? Why must you fear the sight of him? Why do you let him see that you fear it? Why do you make him aware that you are using the power his love gives you over him? Would you dare refuse to see him if you did not know very well that he distinguishes your severity from discourtesy? But why must you be severe with him? From a person such as you, Madam, everything is a favor except indifference.”
>

> 
> “Yet I do reproach you for it, Madam,” he replied, “and my reproaches are well founded. Why not see him if he has said nothing to you? But, Madam, he has spoken to you; if his silence alone had revealed his love to you, it would not have made so great an impression upon you. You could not tell me the whole truth; you concealed the greater part of it from me; you even repented of the little you confessed to me, and you did not have the strength to continue. I am more unhappy than I believed, and I am the unhappiest of all men. You are my wife; I love you as my beloved, and I see you love another. That other man is the most lovable at court, and he sees you every day; he knows that you love him. Ah! I could believe,” he exclaimed, “that you would overcome the love you have for him. >

I no longer find myself worthy of you; you no longer seem worthy of me. I adore you; I hate you. I offend you; I ask your forgiveness. I admire you; I am ashamed to admire you.
