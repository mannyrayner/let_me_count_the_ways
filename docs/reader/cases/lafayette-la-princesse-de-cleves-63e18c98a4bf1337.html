How the scores were assigned

Monsieur de Clèves directly presents his former love for his wife as a true, exceptionally deep emotional state. The imperfect « je vous aimais » is retrospective but still plainly avowal-like and truth-conditional. In this comparative clause he does not undertake or renew a relational commitment; instead, he contrasts his past marital love with the allegedly self-interested professions of seducers. Although the larger speech is emotionally uncontrolled and delivered near death, the highlighted words function as part of a reasoned accusation and self-description rather than as a reflex-like affective discharge.

Vous m'avez éclairci trop tard; mais ce me sera toujours un soulagement d'emporter la pensée que vous êtes digne de l'estime que j'aie eue pour vous. Je vous prie que je puisse encore avoir la consolation de croire que ma mémoire vous sera chère, et que, s'il eût dépendu de vous, vous eussiez eu pour moi les sentiments que vous avez pour un autre.

English translation

AI-generated analytical aid, supplied to the annotator; scope: wide context.

As soon as she was at Blois, where the court then was, Monsieur de Nemours could not keep himself from rejoicing to know that she was in the same place as he. He tried to see her and went every day to Monsieur de Clèves’s house, on the pretext of inquiring after his health; but it was in vain. She never left her husband’s room and was in violent grief over the condition in which she saw him. Monsieur de Nemours was in despair that she was so afflicted; he easily judged how greatly this affliction renewed the affection she had for Monsieur de Clèves, and how greatly this affection dangerously diverted her from the passion she had in her heart. This thought caused him mortal anguish for some time; but the extremity of Monsieur de Clèves’s illness opened new hopes to him. He saw that Madame de Clèves might perhaps be free to follow her inclination, and that he might find in the future a succession of lasting happiness and pleasures. He could not endure this thought, so much agitation and rapture did it cause him, and he turned his mind away from it for fear of finding himself too unhappy if he should lose his hopes. Meanwhile, Monsieur de Clèves had been almost given up by the physicians. On one of the last days of his illness, after spending a very distressing night, he said toward morning that he wished to rest. Madame de Clèves remained alone in his room; it seemed to her that, instead of resting, he was very uneasy. She approached and came to kneel before his bed, her face entirely covered with tears. Monsieur de Clèves had resolved not to show her the violent resentment he felt against her; but the care she lavished on him and her affliction, which sometimes seemed genuine to him and which he also sometimes regarded as signs of dissimulation and perfidy, caused such conflicting and painful feelings in him that he could not contain them within himself. —You shed many tears, Madame, he said to her, for a death that you are causing and that cannot give you the grief you display. I am no longer in a condition to reproach you, he continued, in a voice weakened by illness and sorrow; but I am dying of the cruel pain you have caused me. Was it necessary that an action as extraordinary as your speaking to me at Coulommiers should have so little consequence? Why enlighten me about the passion you had for Monsieur de Nemours if your virtue did not extend far enough to resist it? I loved you to the point of being glad to be deceived, I confess it to my shame; I have regretted that false peace from which you roused me. Why did you not leave me in that tranquil blindness enjoyed by so many husbands? I might have remained ignorant all my life that you loved Monsieur de Nemours. I shall die, he added; but know that you make death welcome to me, and that, after depriving me of the esteem and tenderness I had for you, life would fill me with horror. What would I do with life, he went on, whether I spent it with a person whom I loved so greatly and by whom I have been so cruelly deceived, or lived apart from that same person and went so far as a public scandal and acts of violence so contrary to my disposition and to the passion I had for you? It went beyond what you saw of it, Madame; I concealed the greater part of it from you for fear of importuning you or of losing some of your esteem through conduct unbecoming to a husband. In short, I deserved your heart; once again, I die without regret, since I could not have it and can no longer desire it. Farewell, Madame; one day you will regret a man who loved you with a true and legitimate passion. You will feel the sorrow that reasonable people find in such attachments, and you will know the difference between being loved as I loved you and being loved by people who, while professing love for you, seek only the honor of seducing you. But my death will leave you free, he added, and you will be able to make Monsieur de Nemours happy without its costing you the commission of crimes. What does it matter, he went on, what will happen when I am no more, and must I be so weak as to turn my eyes toward it! Madame de Clèves was so far from imagining that her husband could harbor suspicions against her that she listened to all these words without understanding them and with no other idea than that he was reproaching her for her inclination toward Monsieur de Nemours; at last, suddenly emerging from her blindness: —I, guilty of crimes! she exclaimed; the very thought of them is unknown to me. The most austere virtue could inspire no conduct other than mine; and I have never done anything without wishing that you had witnessed it. —Would you have wished, Monsieur de Clèves replied, looking at her disdainfully, that I had witnessed the nights you spent with Monsieur de Nemours? Ah! Madame, can it be you of whom I speak when I speak of a woman who has spent nights with a man? —No, Monsieur, she replied; no, it is not of me that you speak. I have never spent either nights or moments with Monsieur de Nemours. He has never seen me in private; I have never permitted him to approach me or listened to him, and I would swear every oath to it... —Say no more, Monsieur de Clèves interrupted; false oaths or a confession might cause me equal pain. Madame de Clèves could not answer; her tears and sorrow deprived her of speech; at last, making an effort: —At least look at me; listen to me, she said to him. If only my own interest were at stake, I would endure these reproaches; but your life is at stake. Listen to me, for your own sake: it is impossible that, speaking with such truth, I should not convince you of my innocence. —Would to God that you could convince me of it! he exclaimed; but what can you tell me? Did Monsieur de Nemours not come to Coulommiers with his sister? And had he not spent the two preceding nights with you in the forest garden? —If that is my crime, she replied, it is easy for me to justify myself. I do not ask you to believe me; but believe all your servants, and find out whether I went into the forest garden on the day before Monsieur de Nemours came to Coulommiers, and whether, on the evening before that, I did not leave it two hours earlier than was my custom. She then told him how she had thought she saw someone in that garden. She admitted to him that she had believed it was Monsieur de Nemours. She spoke to him with such assurance, and truth is so readily believed even when it is not plausible, that Monsieur de Clèves was almost convinced of her innocence. —I do not know, he said to her, whether I ought to allow myself to believe you. I feel so near death that I do not wish to see anything that might make me regret life. You have enlightened me too late; but it will always be a comfort to me to carry away with me the thought that you are worthy of the esteem I have had for you. I beg you to allow me still to have the consolation of believing that my memory will be dear to you, and that, had it depended upon you, you would have had for me the feelings you have for another.
