How the scores were assigned

The old woman’s “jeg elsker dig til døden” is principally an unequivocal avowal that her love persists despite her husband’s disfigurement. “Til døden” gives the avowal a weak vow-like implication of lifelong devotion, but the clause does not clearly undertake a new obligation. Narrative situation

The original annotator’s interpretation of the supplied context.

The husband has deliberately disfigured himself and asks whether his wife still loves him. Her answer reassures him by affirming continued attraction and love unto death. It culminates an idealized account of reciprocal marital constancy in which each spouse responds to the other’s disfigurement or disability with sacrificial solidarity. The framing and heightened delivery intensify the avowal but do not displace its central truth-conditional force.

But after speaking of many kinds of love, the monk Vendt tells of yet another kind and says: For so intoxicating is a peculiar kind of love! The young master and mistress have just come home, their long honeymoon is over, and they retire for the night. A shooting star flared above their roof. In summer the young master and mistress walked together and never left each other’s side. They picked yellow, red, and blue flowers, which they gave to one another; they watched the grass move in the wind and heard the birds singing in the forests, and every word they spoke was like a caress. In winter they drove with bells on the horses, and the sky was blue, and high above, the stars rushed across the eternal plains. Thus many, many years passed. The young master and mistress had three children, and their hearts loved one another as on the first day beneath the first kiss. Then the proud master contracted his illness, the illness that chained him to his bed for so long and put his wife’s patience to so severe a test. The day he recovered and rose from his bed, he did not recognize himself; the illness had disfigured him and taken his hair. He suffered and brooded. Then one morning he said: Now you surely do not love me anymore? But his wife, blushing, threw her arms around him and kissed him as passionately as in the springtime of youth, and answered: I, I love, love you always. I never forget that it was me and no one else whom you chose, and who became so happy. And she went into her chamber and cut all the yellow hair from her head so as to resemble her husband, whom she loved. And again many, many years passed; the young master and mistress grew old, and their children were grown. They shared every happiness as before; in summer they still walked in the fields and watched the grass ripple, and in winter they wrapped themselves in their furs and drove beneath the starry sky. And their hearts continued to be warm and glad, as if from wondrous wine. Then the mistress became paralyzed. The old mistress could not walk on her feet; she had to be pulled in a wheeled chair, and the master himself pulled her. But the mistress suffered so unspeakably in her misfortune, and her face acquired deep furrows of sorrow. Then one day she said: Now I should like to die. I am so paralyzed and hideous, and your face is so handsome; you can no longer kiss me, and you cannot love me as before. But the master embraces her, red with emotion, and answers: I, I love you more, more than my life, my dear; I love you as on the first day, the first moment when you gave me the rose. Do you remember? You held out the rose to me and looked at me with your beautiful eyes; the rose was fragrant like you, you blushed like it, and I became intoxicated in all my senses. The master goes into his chamber, pours acid on his face to disfigure it, and says to his wife: I had the misfortune of getting acid on my face; my cheeks are covered with burns, and surely you do not love me anymore? You are more handsome than any man on earth; your voice still makes my heart burn even today, and I love you unto death.
