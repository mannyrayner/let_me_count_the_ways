A profound silence reigned around Oswald and Corinne; the guides themselves had withdrawn into the distance; and since near the crater there is neither animal, nor insect, nor plant, nothing could be heard there but the hissing of the agitated flame. Nevertheless, a sound from the city reached even that place; it was the sound of bells making themselves heard through the air: perhaps they were celebrating a death; perhaps they were announcing a birth; no matter, they caused a gentle emotion in the travelers. “Dear Oswald,” said Corinne, “let us leave this desert, let us go back down toward the living; my soul is ill at ease here. All other mountains, in bringing us nearer to heaven, seem to raise us above earthly life; but here I feel only turmoil and terror: I seem to see nature treated like a criminal, and condemned, like a depraved being, no longer to feel the beneficent breath of its Creator. This is surely not the abode of the good; let us go away.” Heavy rain was falling while Corinne and Lord Nelvil went back down toward the plain. At every moment their torches were on the verge of going out. The lazzaroni accompanied them, uttering continual cries that might inspire terror in anyone who did not know that this was their habitual manner. But these men are sometimes stirred by a surplus of life with which they do not know what to do, because they combine idleness and violence in equal measure. Their physiognomy, more strongly marked than their character, seems to indicate a kind of vivacity in which the mind and the heart play no part. Oswald, anxious lest the rain should harm Corinne, lest their light should fail them, and, finally, lest she should be exposed to some danger, concerned himself with her alone; and this very tender concern gradually restored his soul from the state into which the confidence he had made to her had thrown it. They found their carriage again at the foot of the mountain; they did not stop at the ruins of Herculaneum, which have, as it were, been buried anew so as not to demolish the town of Portici, which is built over that ancient city. They arrived in Naples toward midnight, and Corinne, on taking leave of Lord Nelvil, promised to give him the story of her life the following morning. CHAPTER II Indeed, the next morning Corinne resolved to impose upon herself the effort she had promised; and although the more intimate knowledge she had gained of Oswald’s character redoubled her anxiety, she left her room carrying what she had written, trembling, yet resolved to give it to him. She entered the sitting room of the inn where they were both staying. Oswald was there and had just received letters from England. One of those letters lay on the mantelpiece, and the handwriting struck Corinne so forcibly that, in indescribable agitation, she asked him from whom it came. “It is from Lady Edgermond,” Oswald replied.—“You correspond with her?” Corinne interrupted.—“Lord Edgermond was my father’s friend,” Oswald resumed; “and since chance has led me to speak to you of her, I shall not conceal from you that my father had thought that it might one day be suitable for me to marry Lucile Edgermond, her daughter.”—“Great God!” cried Corinne, and she sank onto a chair, almost fainting. “Whence comes this cruel emotion?” said Lord Nelvil; “what can you fear from me, Corinne, when I love you with idolatrous devotion? If my father, as he was dying, had asked me to marry Lucile, I should doubtless not consider myself free, and I should have kept away from your irresistible charm; but he did no more than advise me to make this marriage, writing to me himself that he could not judge Lucile, since she was still only a child. I myself have seen her only once; she was scarcely twelve years old then. I entered into no engagement with her mother before leaving; nevertheless, the uncertainty and turmoil you may have noticed in my conduct arose solely from this wish of my father’s: before knowing you, I wished to be able to fulfill it, fleeting though it was, as a kind of expiation toward him, as a way of prolonging, after his death, the sway of his will over my resolutions; but you have triumphed over that feeling, you have triumphed over my entire self, and I need only obtain forgiveness for whatever in my conduct must have seemed to you weakness or irresolution. Corinne, one never entirely recovers from the grief I have suffered: it withers hope, it gives one a painful and distressing sense of timidity; destiny has done me so much harm that even when it seems to offer me the greatest good, I still mistrust it. But, dear friend, these anxieties have vanished; I am yours forever, yours! I tell myself that if my father had known you, it is you whom he would have chosen as the companion of my life; it is you…”—“Stop,” cried Corinne, bursting into tears, “I implore you, do not speak to me in this way. —“Why should you oppose,” said Lord Nelvil, “the pleasure I find in uniting you in my thoughts with the memory of my father, in thus blending in my heart all that is dear and sacred to me?”—“You cannot do so,” Corinne interrupted; “Oswald, I know too well that you cannot.”—“Good heavens!” Lord Nelvil resumed, “what have you to tell me? Give me that document which is to contain the story of your life, give it to me.”—“You shall have it,” Corinne resumed; “but I implore you, grant me eight more days, only eight days. What I learned this morning obliges me to add a few more details.”—“What!” said Oswald, “what connection do you have…?”—“Do not require me to answer you now,” Corinne interrupted; “soon you will know everything, and it may be the end, the terrible end of my happiness; but before that moment, I want us to see the happy countryside of Naples together, while our feelings are still gentle, while our souls are still open to this enchanting nature: I want, in some way, in these beautiful places, to consecrate the most solemn period of my life; you must preserve one last memory of me, as I was, as I would always have been, if my heart had guarded itself against loving you. —“Ah! Corinne,” said Oswald, “what do you mean to announce to me with these ominous words? It cannot be that you have anything to tell me that would cool either my tenderness or my admiration. Why, then, prolong for another eight days this anxiety, this mystery, which seems to raise a barrier between us?”—“Dear Oswald, I wish it so,” Corinne replied; “forgive me this last exercise of power; soon you alone will decide for us both; I shall await my fate from your lips without murmuring, if it is cruel; for on this earth I have neither feelings nor ties that condemn me to survive your love.” As she finished these words, she went out, gently pushing back with her hand Oswald, who wished to follow her.
