How the scores were assigned

In “vous saurez combien je vous aime,” Lucien presents his existing love for Ève and David as a fact whose degree his future results will reveal. The utterance is therefore strongly truth-conditional. It makes no distinct relational undertaking, and its controlled role in an extended justification supplies no positive evidence of reflex-like expressive discharge.

His avowal reassures them and rhetorically reframes ambition and eventual success as proof of love. Toutes deux pleurèrent en écoutant l'ambitieux; et, quand il voulut savoir la cause de ce chagrin, elles lui apprirent que tout ce qu'elles possédaient avait été absorbé par le linge de table et de maison, par le trousseau d'Ève, par une multitude d'acquisitions auxquelles n'avait pas pensé David, et qu'elles étaient heureuses d'avoir faites, car l'imprimeur reconnaissait à Ève une dot de dix mille francs. Elle nous accordera bien huit jours, à nous qui t'avons élevé pour elle! Notre union tournera mal si tu n'y es pas... Mais auras-tu assez de mille francs? dit-elle en s'interrompant tout à coup. Quoique ton habit t'aille divinement, tu n'en as qu'un! Tu n'as que deux chemises fines, et les six autres sont en grosse toile. Tu n'as que trois cravates de batiste, les trois autres sont en jaconas commun; et puis tes mouchoirs ne sont pas beaux. Trouveras-tu dans Paris une sœur pour te blanchir ton linge dans la journée où tu en auras besoin? il t'en faut bien davantage. Tu n'as qu'un pantalon de nankin fait cette année, ceux de l'année dernière te sont justes, il faudra donc te faire habiller à Paris, les prix de Paris ne sont pas ceux d'Angoulême. Tu n'as que deux gilets blancs de mettables, j'ai déjà raccommodé les autres. Tiens, je te conseille d'emporter deux mille francs. En ce moment David, qui entrait, parut avoir entendu ces deux derniers mots, car il examina le frère et la sœur en gardant le silence. —Ne me cachez rien, dit-il. —Eh! bien, s'écria Ève, il part avec elle. —Postel, dit madame Chardon en entrant sans voir David, consent à prêter les mille francs, mais pour six mois seulement, et il veut une lettre de change de toi acceptée par ton beau-frère, car il dit que tu n'offres aucune garantie. La mère se retourna, vit son gendre, et ces quatre personnes gardèrent un profond silence. La famille Chardon sentait combien elle avait abusé de David. Tous étaient honteux. Une larme roula dans les yeux de l'imprimeur. —Tu ne seras donc pas à mon mariage? dit-il, tu ne resteras donc pas avec nous? Et moi qui ai dissipé tout ce que j'avais! Ah, Lucien, moi qui apportais à Ève ses pauvres petits bijoux de mariée, je ne savais pas, dit-il en essuyant ses yeux et tirant des écrins de sa poche, avoir à regretter de les avoir achetés. Lucien inclina mollement et tristement la tête en ajoutant un moment après:—Ne me jugez pas mal, mes anges aimés. Malgré la distance, mon âme ne sera-t-elle pas ici? la pensée ne nous réunira-t-elle pas? N'ai-je pas une destinée à accomplir? Les libraires viendront-ils chercher ici mon Archer de Charles IX, et les Marguerites? Un peu plus tôt, un peu plus tard, ne faut-il pas toujours faire ce que je fais aujourd'hui, puis-je jamais rencontrer des circonstances plus favorables? N'est-ce pas toute ma fortune que d'entrer pour mon début à Paris dans le salon de la marquise d'Espard? —Il a raison, dit Ève. Vous-même ne me disiez-vous pas qu'il devait aller promptement à Paris? David prit Ève par la main, l'emmena dans cet étroit cabinet où elle dormait depuis sept années, et lui dit à l'oreille:—Il a besoin de deux mille francs, disais-tu, mon amour? Postel n'en prête que mille. Ève regarda son prétendu par un regard affreux qui disait toutes ses souffrances. —Écoute, mon Ève adorée, nous allons mal commencer la vie. Donner mille francs à ton frère, c'est donner notre pain, compromettre notre tranquillité. Si j'étais seul, je sais ce que je ferais; mais nous sommes deux. Décide. Ève éperdue se jeta dans les bras de son amant, le baisa tendrement et lui dit à l'oreille, tout en pleurs:—Fais comme si tu étais seul, je travaillerai pour regagner cette somme! Quand les deux amis remontèrent, ils surprirent Ève et sa mère à genoux, qui priaient Dieu. Si elles savaient combien d'espérances le retour devait réaliser, elles sentaient en ce moment tout ce qu'elles perdaient dans cet adieu; car elles trouvaient le bonheur à venir payé trop cher par une absence qui allait briser leur vie, et les jeter dans mille craintes sur les destinées de Lucien. —Si jamais tu oubliais cette scène, dit David à l'oreille de Lucien, tu serais le dernier des hommes. L'imprimeur jugea sans doute ces graves paroles nécessaires, l'influence de madame de Bargeton ne l'épouvantait pas moins que la funeste mobilité de caractère qui pouvait tout aussi bien jeter Lucien dans une mauvaise comme dans une bonne voie. Ève eut bientôt fait le paquet de Lucien. Ce Fernand Cortès littéraire emportait peu de chose. Il garda sur lui sa meilleure redingote, son meilleur gilet et l'une de ses deux chemises fines. Tout son linge, son fameux habit, ses effets et ses manuscrits formèrent un si mince paquet, que, pour le cacher aux regards de madame de Bargeton, David proposa de l'envoyer par la diligence à son correspondant, un marchand de papier, auquel il écrirait de le tenir à la disposition de Lucien.

Under a promise of profound secrecy, Ève and her mother listened to Lucien’s confidences. Both wept as they listened to the ambitious young man; and when he wished to know the cause of their sorrow, they told him that everything they possessed had been absorbed by table and household linen, by Ève’s trousseau, and by a multitude of purchases that David had not thought of and that they were glad to have made, for the printer acknowledged that Ève brought a dowry of ten thousand francs. Lucien then told them of his idea of taking out a loan, and Madame Chardon undertook to go and ask Monsieur Postel for a thousand francs for one year. “But, Lucien,” said Ève with a pang at her heart, “then you will not be at my wedding? Oh! Come back; I shall wait a few days! Surely she will let you come back here in a fortnight, once you have accompanied her! Surely she will grant us eight days—to us who raised you for her! Our marriage will turn out badly if you are not there... But will a thousand francs be enough for you?” she said, suddenly interrupting herself. “Although your coat suits you divinely, you have only one! You have only two fine shirts, and the other six are of coarse linen. You have only three batiste cravats; the other three are of ordinary jaconet; and besides, your handkerchiefs are not handsome. Will you find a sister in Paris to wash and whiten your linen the very day you need it? You need much more of it. You have only one pair of nankeen trousers made this year; last year’s are tight on you, so you will have to have clothes made in Paris, and Paris prices are not Angoulême prices. You have only two white waistcoats fit to wear; I have already mended the others. Listen, I advise you to take two thousand francs with you.” At that moment David, who was coming in, appeared to have heard those last two words, for he looked at the brother and sister and remained silent. “Do not hide anything from me,” he said. “Well!” cried Ève, “he is leaving with her.” “Postel,” said Madame Chardon, entering without seeing David, “agrees to lend the thousand francs, but for six months only, and he wants a bill of exchange from you accepted by your brother-in-law, for he says that you offer no security.” The mother turned, saw her son-in-law, and the four of them remained in profound silence. The Chardon family felt how greatly they had taken advantage of David. They were all ashamed. A tear welled in the printer’s eyes. “Then you will not be at my wedding?” he said. “Then you will not stay with us? And I, who have spent everything I had! Ah, Lucien, as I was bringing Ève her poor little bridal jewels, I did not know,” he said, wiping his eyes and drawing jewel cases from his pocket, “that I would have cause to regret having bought them.” He placed several morocco-covered boxes on the table before his mother-in-law. “Why do you think so much of me?” said Ève with an angelic smile that softened her words. “Dear mother,” said the printer, “go and tell Monsieur Postel that I consent to give my signature, for I can see from your face, Lucien, that you are quite determined to leave.” Lucien bowed his head listlessly and sadly, adding a moment later, “Do not judge me harshly, my beloved angels.” He took hold of Ève and David, kissed them, drew them close to him, and clasped them as he said, “Wait for the results, and you will know how much I love you. David, what use would our loftiness of thought be if it did not allow us to disregard the petty ceremonies in which laws entangle feelings? Despite the distance, will my soul not be here? Will thought not unite us? Have I not a destiny to fulfill? Will the booksellers come here in search of my Archer of Charles IX and the Daisies? A little sooner or a little later, must I not in any event do what I am doing today? Could I ever encounter more favorable circumstances? Is it not my entire fortune to make my Paris debut in the Marquise d’Espard’s salon?” “He is right,” said Ève. “Did you not tell me yourself that he had to go to Paris promptly?” David took Ève by the hand, led her into the narrow room where she had slept for seven years, and whispered in her ear, “He needs two thousand francs, you said, my love? Postel is lending only a thousand.” Ève looked at her betrothed with a terrible gaze that expressed all her suffering. “Listen, my adored Ève, we are going to begin life badly. Yes, my expenses have absorbed everything I possessed. I have only two thousand francs left, and half of that is indispensable to keep the printing house going. To give your brother a thousand francs is to give away our bread, to jeopardize our peace of mind. If I were alone, I know what I would do; but there are two of us. You decide.” Distraught, Ève threw herself into her lover’s arms, kissed him tenderly, and whispered in his ear, all in tears, “Act as though you were alone; I shall work to earn that sum back!” Despite the most ardent kiss ever exchanged by two betrothed lovers, David left Ève dejected and returned to Lucien. “Do not grieve,” he told him. “You shall have your two thousand francs.” “Go and see Postel,” said Madame Chardon, “for you must both sign the paper.” When the two friends came back upstairs, they surprised Ève and her mother on their knees, praying to God. Although they knew how many hopes his return was to fulfill, at that moment they felt all that they were losing in this farewell; for they found the happiness to come too dearly purchased by an absence that was about to shatter their lives and plunge them into a thousand fears concerning Lucien’s fate. “If you ever forgot this scene,” David whispered in Lucien’s ear, “you would be the lowest of men.” The printer doubtless judged these grave words necessary: Madame de Bargeton’s influence alarmed him no less than the disastrous fickleness of character that might just as readily cast Lucien onto a bad path as onto a good one. Ève soon finished packing Lucien’s bundle. This literary Hernán Cortés was taking little with him. He kept on his best frock coat, his best waistcoat, and one of his two fine shirts. All his linen, his famous coat, his belongings, and his manuscripts formed so slight a bundle that, in order to conceal it from Madame de Bargeton’s eyes, David proposed sending it by stagecoach to his correspondent, a paper merchant, to whom he would write instructing him to hold it at Lucien’s disposal.
