Amatelo oggi ancora, non importa; è giusto che lo amiate; dovrò io cancellare la sua imagine dal vostro cuore. E di repente proruppe: --Sono felice; sono felice di vedervi presso di me; vi siete ricordata che vi ho offerta la mia amicizia in un caso estremo, e questo mi consola di molti dolori.... Ah vi assicuro, Loredana, che non sono più lo sventato che avete conosciuto un giorno! Vi amo teneramente e spero di potervi rassicurare.... Ella troncò le sue parole con un gesto. --No,--rispose.--Non vi amerò mai. Si guardò intorno, e vedendo la coppa, la portò alle labbra che sentiva arse da un'interna febbre. --Ascoltatemi,--proseguì imperiosamente.--Sono qui, non perchè vi ami, non perchè io creda alla vostra amicizia, ma perchè voglio e devo perdermi. --Loredana, Loredana, per carità!--interruppe Berto. --Devo perdermi. No, io non posso accettare questo sacrificio; è una cosa orrenda; io devo lasciarlo e in maniera ch'egli non mi cerchi più, non mi desideri più.... Non voglio far male a sua madre, che per me ha già tanto sofferto.... Se morisse, ah se morisse, quale rimorso, quale vergogna!... E un giorno egli si sveglierebbe da questa follia; e tra me e lui, sempre, sempre, io vedrei il cadavere di sua madre.... Bisogna che io gli impedisca di disonorarsi! Berto, ritto in piedi, ascoltava con un senso di maraviglia la giovane, che parlava velocemente agitata da violento orgasmo; alla prima pallidezza era subentrato un rossore febbrile che le imporporava le guance, le faceva brillare intensamente gli occhi, le invermigliava le labbra dando loro un color di vivo sangue. Quante cose ho imparato, spaventevoli! E allora ho pensato che avevano ragione. Loredana rabbrividì; raccolta la pelliccia, cercò degli occhi il cappello. Ma mentre stava per riprenderlo, si arrestò quasi folgorata da un pensiero. Dove andava? A casa, sua? Candriani was not listening to her; drunk with joy, now able to gaze at her with all the intensity of a desire no longer restrained, he gazed avidly over her whole body, from her head to her little feet, whose tips peeped out from beneath the very hem of her skirt. “I shall make you forget Filippo,” he said suddenly. “You will no longer love him; I am free and alone, I can devote my entire life to you.... Love him still today, it does not matter; it is right that you should love him; it will be for me to erase his image from your heart.” And all at once he burst out: “I am happy; I am happy to see you here beside me; you remembered that I offered you my friendship in a desperate situation, and that consoles me for many sorrows.... Ah, I assure you, Loredana, that I am no longer the thoughtless man you once knew! I love you tenderly and hope that I may reassure you....” She cut off his words with a gesture. “No,” she replied. “I shall never love you.” She looked around, and, seeing the goblet, raised it to lips that she felt were parched by an inward fever. “Listen to me,” she continued imperiously. “I am here not because I love you, not because I believe in your friendship, but because I wish and must ruin myself.” “Loredana, Loredana, for pity’s sake!” Berto interrupted. “I must ruin myself. He is about to commit a crime. His sick mother has asked him to leave me, and he has refused.... No, I cannot accept this sacrifice; it is a horrible thing; I must leave him, and in such a way that he will no longer seek me, no longer desire me.... I do not wish to hurt his mother, who has already suffered so much because of me.... If she were to die—ah, if she were to die—what remorse, what shame!... And one day he would awaken from this madness; and between him and me, always, always, I would see his mother’s corpse.... I must prevent him from dishonoring himself!” Berto, standing upright, listened with a sense of wonder to the young woman, who spoke rapidly, agitated by violent excitement; her initial pallor had given way to a feverish flush that crimsoned her cheeks, made her eyes shine intensely, and reddened her lips, giving them the color of living blood. She pleased him so much thus stretched out in the armchair and sunk into the white fur that Berto bent down once more to kiss her hands. Loredana pushed him away. “I wanted to kill myself,” she continued, “but it would have caused a fresh scandal; they might have accused Filippo of my death.... Ah, how malicious everyone is!... I have been told to my face that I am your mistress, that my honesty is worth nothing because no one believes me, that a man is a thief when everyone calls him a thief.... How many dreadful things I have learned! And then I thought that they were right. The only person who did not doubt me was Filippo; he believes in my love and in my honesty, which everyone laughs at; and so he too must cease to believe, in order that he may be saved.... I thought that, since they call me your mistress, every effort is useless, and I shall never be able to free myself from this accusation....” “Do not speak so,” Berto interrupted. “It does thee too much harm....” She stared at him with wide-open eyes; that “thee” seemed to her more brutal than a kiss that had suddenly closed her mouth; but Berto noticed it and added: “I beg your pardon; I did not mean to offend you. I love you, and I was unable to master myself.” “No,” said Loredana, rising, “let me go!” Berto dared to lay a hand on her. “I beg you,” he murmured, “stay a little longer.....” Loredana shuddered; gathering up the fur, she searched for her hat with her eyes. But just as she was about to take it up again, she stopped, as though struck by a thought. Where was she going? To her own home? Filippo would take her back. To Filippo’s home? His mother would die of it. And by then Filippo must already have read the few lines Loredana had left him: “I shall never forget thee; I shall always love thee; the farther away I may seem to thee, the more wholly thine I shall be....” And after this, was she to return to him, with bowed head, like a repentant schoolgirl, and forever too late to be forgiven? Once more she saw that accursed woman with goatlike feet, half-naked, who, with her right arm raised, seemed to command her to stop. She turned, and on the other side saw Berto, who dared not move for fear of detaining her, dared not speak for fear of frightening her, and kept watching her, seeking to divine from her gestures the decision she would make. She returned to her armchair, let herself fall into it, and said not a word. There followed in this manner an anguished silence of several minutes, during which Loredana and Berto stared keenly at one another, motionless, almost as though searching each other’s souls; but Berto could endure it no longer, approached her again, and seized her hands. “Stay!” he said, his voice veiled by passion. “Stay! I implore thee!
