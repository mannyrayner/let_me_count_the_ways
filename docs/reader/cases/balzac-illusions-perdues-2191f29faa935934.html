Narrative situation

The original annotator’s interpretation of the supplied context.

The avowal forms part of a calculated theatrical performance. Lucien adopts an operatic pose, deliberately produces tears, and speaks precisely when he sees that Louise has noticed them. This strongly suggests strategic seduction or manipulation, though the words still present former love as true. His subsequent invocation of recovered memories and youthful illusions leaves some possibility that real feeling accompanies the performance.

“Your Grace was almost a prophet,” he said in a charming voice, “and I shall endeavor to make you one altogether. I count myself fortunate to have come here this evening, since I am able to pay you my respects.” Lucien drew His Lordship into a conversation that lasted ten minutes. All the women looked upon Lucien as a phenomenon. His unexpected impertinence had left Madame du Châtelet without voice or answer. Seeing Lucien the object of every woman’s admiration; following, from group to group, the whispered account each gave the others of the exchanged remarks in which Lucien had as good as crushed her while appearing to disdain her, she felt a pang at her heart from wounded pride. “If he did not come tomorrow, after that remark, what a scandal!” she thought. “Where does this pride of his come from? Could Mademoiselle des Touches be in love with him?…” “He is so handsome!—They say she rushed to his home in Paris the day after the actress’s death!… Perhaps he came to save his brother-in-law and happened to find himself behind our carriage at Mansle through an accident of travel. That morning, Lucien looked Sixte and me over in a most singular fashion.” A myriad thoughts arose, and, unfortunately for Louise, she yielded to them as she watched Lucien conversing with the bishop as though he were the king of the drawing room: he bowed to no one and waited for people to come to him, sweeping his gaze about with varied expressions and with an ease worthy of de Marsay, his model. He did not leave the prelate to go and greet Monsieur de Sénonches, who appeared a short distance away. At the end of ten minutes, Louise could bear it no longer. She rose, walked over to the bishop, and said to him, “What are they saying to you, then, Your Grace, to make you smile so often?” Lucien stepped back a few paces, discreetly leaving Madame de Châtelet with the prelate. “Ah! Madame la Comtesse, this young man is very witty!… He was explaining to me how he owed all his strength to you…” “I, madame, am not ungrateful!…” said Lucien, casting her a reproachful glance that delighted the countess. “Let us come to an understanding,” she said, drawing Lucien back toward her with a gesture of her fan. “Come with His Lordship, this way!… His Grace shall be our judge.” And she indicated the boudoir, drawing the bishop into it. “She is putting His Lordship to a strange use,” said a woman from the Chandour camp, loudly enough to be heard. “Our judge!…” said Lucien, looking in turn at the prelate and the prefect’s wife. “Then there is to be a guilty party?” Louise de Nègrepelisse sat down on the sofa in her former boudoir. After seating Lucien beside her and His Lordship on her other side, she began to speak. Lucien did his former friend the honor, the surprise, and the kindness of not listening. He assumed the attitude and gestures of Pasta in _Tancredi_ when she is about to say, _O patria!_… With his countenance he performed the famous _del Rizzo_ cavatina. Finally, Coralie’s pupil managed to bring a few tears to his eyes. “Ah! Louise, how I loved you!” he whispered in her ear, heedless of the prelate and of the conversation, the moment he saw that the countess had noticed his tears. “Dry your eyes, or you would ruin me here once again,” she said, turning toward him in an aside that shocked the bishop. “And once is enough,” Lucien promptly replied. “That remark from Madame d’Espard’s cousin would dry all the tears of a Magdalen. My God!… For a moment I had recovered my memories, my illusions, my twenty years, and you…” His Lordship abruptly returned to the drawing room, realizing that his dignity might be compromised between these two former lovers. Everyone made a show of leaving the prefect’s wife and Lucien alone in the boudoir. But a quarter of an hour later, Sixte, displeased by the talk, the laughter, and the pacing about at the threshold of the boudoir, entered it with a more than anxious air and found Lucien and Louise in a state of great animation. “Madame,” Sixte whispered in his wife’s ear, “you, who know Angoulême better than I do, should you not be giving some thought to the prefect’s wife and to the government?” “My dear,” said Louise, looking her responsible publisher up and down with a haughty air that made him tremble, “I am discussing with Monsieur de Rubempré matters of importance to you. We are concerned with saving an inventor who is on the point of becoming the victim of the basest maneuvers, and you will help us do so… As for what these ladies may think of me, you shall see how I am going to conduct myself so as to freeze the venom on their tongues.” She left the boudoir leaning on Lucien’s arm and led him to sign the contract, openly parading their association with the audacity of a great lady. “Shall we sign together?…” she said, handing Lucien the pen. Lucien allowed her to show him the place where she had just signed, so that their signatures would be side by side. “Monsieur de Sénonches, would you have recognized Monsieur de Rubempré?” said the countess, forcing the insolent huntsman to bow to Lucien. She led Lucien back into the drawing room and placed him between herself and Zéphirine on the formidable sofa in the center. Then, like a queen upon her throne, she began, at first in a low voice, an obviously epigrammatic conversation in which several of her former friends and several women who were paying court to her joined. Soon Lucien, now the hero of a circle, was prompted by the countess to speak of Parisian life, upon which he improvised a satire with incredible verve, interspersed with anecdotes about famous people, true conversational delicacies for which provincials are exceedingly greedy. His wit was admired as the man himself had been admired. Madame la Comtesse Sixte triumphed so patiently in Lucien, handled him so skillfully like a woman enchanted with her choice, supplied him with rejoinders with such aptness, and solicited approval for him with such compromising glances, that several women began to see in the coincidence of Louise’s and Lucien’s return a deep love that had fallen victim to some mutual misunderstanding. Resentment had perhaps brought about the ill-fated Châtelet marriage, against which a reaction was now setting in.
