“All Moscow talks of nothing but war. One of my two brothers is already abroad; the other is with the Guards, who are setting out for the frontier. Our dear Emperor has left Petersburg and, so it is said, intends himself to expose his precious life to the hazards of war. God grant that the Corsican monster, who is destroying the peace of Europe, may be struck down by the angel whom the Almighty, in His mercy, has given us as sovereign. Not to mention my brothers, this war has deprived me of a relationship most dear to my heart. I speak of young Nicholas Rostov, who, in his enthusiasm, could not endure inactivity and has left the university to enlist in the army. Well, dear Marie, I shall confess to you that, despite his extreme youth, his departure for the army has been a great sorrow to me. The young man of whom I spoke to you this summer has so much nobility, such genuine youth as one so rarely encounters in the age in which we live among our twenty-year-old old men. Above all, he has so much frankness and heart. He is so pure and poetic that my relations with him, however fleeting they may have been, have been one of the sweetest joys of my poor heart, which has already suffered so much. One day I shall tell you about our farewells and everything that was said as he was leaving. It is all still too fresh. Ah! dear friend, you are fortunate not to know these joys and these so poignant sorrows. You are fortunate, since the latter are usually the stronger! I know perfectly well that Count Nicholas is too young ever to become anything more to me than a friend, but this sweet friendship, these so poetic and so pure relations, were a need of my heart. But let us speak of it no more. The great news of the day, which occupies all Moscow, is the death of old Count Bezukhov and his inheritance. Just imagine: the three princesses received very little, Prince Vasili nothing, and it is Monsieur Pierre who has inherited everything and, into the bargain, has been recognized as a legitimate son and consequently as Count Bezukhov and the possessor of the finest fortune in Russia. It is said that Prince Vasili played a very ugly part in this whole affair and that he went back to Petersburg quite crestfallen. “I confess to you that I understand very little of all these matters of legacies and wills; what I do know is that, since the young man whom we all knew simply as Monsieur Pierre became Count Bezukhov and the possessor of one of the greatest fortunes in Russia, I have been greatly amused to observe the changes in the tone and manners of the mothers burdened with marriageable daughters, and of the young ladies themselves, toward this individual, who, incidentally, has always seemed to me a sorry fellow. Since for the past two years people have amused themselves by giving me prospective husbands whom, more often than not, I do not know, the matrimonial chronicle of Moscow is making me Countess Bezukhov. But you understand very well that I have no desire whatsoever to become her. Speaking of marriage, do you know that only very recently the aunt-in-general, Anna Mikhailovna, confided to me, under the seal of the greatest secrecy, a marriage plan for you? It is none other than Prince Vasili’s son Anatole, whom they would like to reform by marrying him to a rich and distinguished person, and it is upon you that the parents’ choice has fallen. I do not know how you will view the matter, but I thought it my duty to warn you of it. He is said to be very handsome and a very bad lot; that is all I have been able to learn about him. “But enough chatter of this sort. I am finishing my second sheet, and Mamma has sent for me to go and dine at the Apraksins’. Read the mystical book I am sending you, which is all the rage among us. Although there are things in this book difficult for the feeble human understanding to grasp, it is an admirable book, the reading of which calms and elevates the soul. Farewell. My respects to your father and my compliments to Mademoiselle Bourienne. I embrace you as I love you. Julie.” “P.S. Give me news of your brother and his charming little wife.” The princess thought, smiled pensively—at which her face, illuminated by her radiant eyes, was completely transformed—and, suddenly rising slightly, walked heavily over to the table. She took out some paper, and her hand began moving rapidly over it. This is what she wrote in reply: “My dear and excellent friend. Your letter of the thirteenth has given me great joy. So you still love me, my poetic Julie. Absence, of which you say so much evil, has therefore not had its usual effect on you. You complain of absence—what should I say, if I dared complain, deprived as I am of all those who are dear to me? Ah! if we did not have religion to console us, life would be very sad. Why do you suppose that I look sternly upon you when you speak to me of your affection for the young man? In that respect I am strict only with myself. I understand these feelings in others and, though I cannot approve of them, never having felt them, I do not condemn them. It merely seems to me that Christian love, love of one’s neighbor, love for one’s enemies, is more meritorious, sweeter, and more beautiful than are the feelings that the fine eyes of a young man can inspire in a poetic and loving young girl such as you. “The news of Count Bezukhov’s death reached us before your letter, and my father was deeply affected by it. He says that the count was the penultimate representative of the great century and that now it is his turn, but that he will do all he can to make his turn come as late as possible. May God preserve us from this terrible misfortune! I cannot share your opinion of Pierre, whom I knew as a child. He always seemed to me to have an excellent heart, and that is the quality I esteem most in people. As for his inheritance and the part Prince Vasili played in it, it is very sad for them both. Ah! dear friend, the words of our divine Savior, that it is easier for a camel to pass through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the kingdom of God, are terribly true; I pity Prince Vasili, and I am even sorrier for Pierre. So young and burdened with such wealth—how many temptations he will have to undergo! If I were asked what I desired most in the world, it would be to be poorer than the poorest of beggars. “A thousand thanks, dear friend, for the work you are sending me, which is so greatly in vogue among you. However, since you tell me that amid several good things there are others that the feeble human understanding cannot grasp, it seems to me rather pointless to occupy oneself with unintelligible reading, which for that very reason could bear no fruit. I have never been able to understand the passion certain people have for muddling their understanding by devoting themselves to mystical books, which merely raise doubts in their minds, excite their imagination, and give them a tendency toward exaggeration entirely contrary to Christian simplicity. Let us read the Apostles and the Gospel. Let us not seek to penetrate what mysteries they contain, for how could we, miserable sinners that we are, dare to claim initiation into the terrible and sacred secrets of Providence while we still bear this carnal covering, which raises an impenetrable veil between us and the Eternal? Let us therefore confine ourselves to studying the sublime principles our divine Savior left us for our conduct here below; let us seek to conform to them and follow them; let us persuade ourselves that the less scope we give to our feeble human mind, the more pleasing it is to God, Who rejects all knowledge not proceeding from Him; that the less we seek to fathom what it has pleased Him to conceal from our knowledge, the sooner He will grant us its discovery through His divine spirit.”
