Les hommes, en général, ont plus d'esprit encore à Venise que dans le reste de l'Italie, parce que le gouvernement, tel qu'il était, leur a plus souvent offert des occasions de penser; mais leur imagination n'est pas naturellement aussi ardente que dans le midi de l'Italie; et la plupart des femmes, quoique très-aimables, ont pris, par l'habitude de vivre dans le monde, un langage de _sentimentalité_ qui, ne gênant en rien la liberté des moeurs, ne fait que mettre de l'affectation dans la galanterie. Le grand mérite des Italiennes, à travers tous leurs torts, c'est de n'avoir aucune vanité: ce mérite est un peu perdu à Venise, où il y a plus de société que dans aucune autre ville d'Italie; car la vanité se développe surtout par la société. On y est applaudi si vite et si souvent, que tous les calculs y sont instantanés, et que, pour le succès, _l'on n'y fait pas de crédit au temps_ d'une minute. Néanmoins on trouvait encore à Venise beaucoup de traces de l'originalité et de la facilité des manières italiennes. Les plus grandes dames recevaient toutes leurs visites dans les cafés de la place Saint-Marc, et cette confusion bizarre empêchait que les salons ne devinssent trop sérieusement une arène pour les prétentions de l'amour-propre.

One embarks on the Brenta to reach Venice, and on both sides of the canal one sees the palaces of the Venetians, large and somewhat dilapidated, like Italian magnificence. They are decorated in a bizarre manner that in no way recalls the taste of antiquity. Venetian architecture bears the mark of commerce with the East; it is a mixture of Moorish and Gothic that arouses curiosity without pleasing the imagination. The poplar, that tree as regular as architecture, borders the canal almost everywhere. The sky is a vivid blue that contrasts with the brilliant green of the countryside; this green is sustained by the excessive abundance of water: sky and earth are thus of two colors so sharply divided that this nature itself seems to have been arranged with a kind of artifice, and one does not find there the mysterious vagueness that makes one love southern Italy. The sight of Venice is more astonishing than pleasing; at first one thinks one sees a submerged city, and reflection is necessary in order to admire the genius of the mortals who conquered this dwelling-place from the waters. Naples is built in an amphitheater by the sea, but since Venice stands on entirely flat ground, the bell towers resemble the mast of a ship that remains motionless amid the waves. A feeling of sadness takes possession of the imagination upon entering Venice. One takes leave of vegetation; one does not see even a fly in this abode; all animals are banished from it, and man alone is there to struggle against the sea. The silence is profound in this city, whose streets are canals, and the sound of oars is the sole interruption of that silence. It is not the countryside, since one does not see a tree there; it is not the city, since one does not hear the slightest movement there; it is not even a ship, since one does not advance: it is a dwelling-place that a storm turns into a prison, for there are times when one can leave neither the city nor one’s home. One finds common people in Venice who have never gone from one district to another, who have not seen Saint Mark’s Square, and for whom the sight of a horse or a tree would be a genuine marvel. Those black gondolas gliding over the canals resemble coffins or cradles, man’s last and first dwelling-places. In the evening one sees passing only the reflection of the lanterns that light the gondolas, for then their black color prevents one from distinguishing them. One would say they were shadows gliding over the water, guided by a little star. In this abode everything is mystery: the government, customs, and love. No doubt there are many delights for the heart and the reason when one succeeds in penetrating all these secrets; but foreigners must find the first impression singularly sad. Corinne, who believed in premonitions, and whose shaken imagination made omens of everything, said to Lord Nelvil: “Whence comes the profound melancholy by which I feel myself seized upon entering this city? Is it not proof that some great misfortune will befall me here?” As she spoke these words, she heard three cannon shots fired from one of the islands in the lagoon. Corinne started at the sound and asked her gondoliers what had caused it. “It is a nun taking the veil,” they replied, “in one of those convents in the middle of the sea. The custom among us is that, at the moment when women pronounce their religious vows, they cast behind them a bouquet of flowers that they carried during the ceremony. It is the sign of renunciation of the world; and the cannon shots you have just heard announced that moment just as we entered Venice.” These words made Corinne shudder. Oswald felt her cold hands in his, and a deathly pallor covered her face. “Dear friend,” he said to her, “how can the simplest chance make so strong an impression upon you?”—“No,” said Corinne, “it is not simple; believe me, the flowers of life have been cast behind me forever.”—“When I love you more than ever,” Oswald interrupted, “when my whole soul is yours...” —“Those thunderbolts of war,” Corinne continued, “whose sound elsewhere announces either victory or death, are here consecrated to celebrating the obscure sacrifice of a young girl. It had been terrible; it had become very mild. It had been courageous; it had become timid. Hatred against it was easily reawakened because it had once been formidable; it was easily overthrown because it was so no longer. It was an aristocracy that greatly sought popular favor, but sought it in the manner of despotism, by entertaining the people but not by enlightening them. Nevertheless, being entertained is a fairly agreeable condition for a people, especially in countries where a taste for the things of the imagination is developed by the climate and the fine arts even in the lowest class of society. The people were not given the coarse pleasures that brutalize them, but music, paintings, improvisers, festivals; and the government tended its subjects there as a sultan does his seraglio. It asked them only, as it would women, not to concern themselves with politics and not to judge those in authority; but, at that price, it promised them much entertainment and even a fair amount of splendor; for the spoils of Constantinople that enrich the churches, the standards of Cyprus and Candia that fly over the public square, and the horses of Corinth delight the people’s eyes, and the winged lion of Saint Mark appears to them the emblem of their glory. Since the system of government forbade its subjects to occupy themselves with political affairs, and the situation of the city made agriculture, walking, and hunting impossible, the Venetians had no interest left but amusement; and so this city was a city of pleasures. The Venetian dialect is gentle and light as a pleasant breath: one cannot conceive how those who resisted the League of Cambrai spoke so supple a language. This dialect is charming when devoted to grace or jesting; but when it is used for graver subjects, when one hears verses about death in those sounds so delicate and almost childlike, one would think that the event, sung in this way, was merely a poetic fiction. Men in Venice generally have even more wit than in the rest of Italy, because the government, such as it was, more often afforded them opportunities to think; but their imagination is not naturally as ardent as in southern Italy; and most of the women, though very amiable, have acquired, through the habit of living in society, a language of “sentimentality” that, while in no way constraining freedom of morals, merely introduces affectation into gallantry. The great merit of Italian women, despite all their faults, is that they have no vanity: this merit is somewhat lost in Venice, where there is more society than in any other city in Italy, for vanity develops above all through society. One is applauded there so quickly and so often that every calculation is instantaneous, and, where success is concerned, “one does not extend time even a minute’s credit.” Nevertheless, one still found in Venice many traces of the originality and ease of Italian manners. The greatest ladies received all their visitors in the cafés of Saint Mark’s Square, and this bizarre mingling prevented drawing rooms from becoming too seriously an arena for the pretensions of self-love.
