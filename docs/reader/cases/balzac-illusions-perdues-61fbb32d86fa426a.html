“Well!” one of them was saying, “that poor Naïs, you know? I do not believe it myself; she has a whole blameless life behind her; she is far too proud to be anything other than Monsieur Chardon’s protectress. But if it is true, I pity her with all my heart.” “She is all the more to be pitied because she is making herself dreadfully ridiculous; for she could be Monsieur Lulu’s mother, as Jacques called him. That little would-be poet is twenty-two at most, and Naïs, between ourselves, is a good forty.” “For my part,” Châtelet was saying, “I find that the very position in which Monsieur de Rubempré was proves Naïs’s innocence. One does not go down on one’s knees to ask again for what one has already had.” “That depends!” said Francis with a roguish air that earned him a disapproving glance from Zéphirine. “But do tell us exactly what happened?” people asked Stanislas as they formed a secret committee in a corner of the drawing room. Stanislas had ended by concocting a little tale full of obscenities, accompanying it with gestures and poses that made the affair look exceedingly incriminating. “It is unbelievable,” they kept repeating. “At noon,” said one of them. “Naïs would have been the last person I should have suspected.” “What is she going to do?” Then came endless comments and suppositions!... Du Châtelet defended Madame de Bargeton; but he defended her so clumsily that he fanned the flames of gossip instead of extinguishing them. Lili, devastated by the fall of the most beautiful angel on the Olympus of Angoulême, went in tears to spread the news at the Bishop’s Palace. When the whole town was most certainly in an uproar, the delighted du Châtelet went to Madame de Bargeton’s, where, alas, there was only a single whist table; he diplomatically asked Naïs to go and talk with him in her boudoir. They both sat down on the little sofa. “You doubtless know,” du Châtelet said to her in a low voice, “what all Angoulême is talking about?...” “No,” she said. “Well,” he resumed, “I am too much your friend to leave you unaware of it. I must enable you to put a stop to slanders doubtless invented by Amélie, who has the effrontery to consider herself your rival. I was coming to see you this morning with that ape Stanislas, who was a few paces ahead of me, when, on reaching there,” he said, pointing to the boudoir door, “he claims to have seen you with Monsieur de Rubempré in a situation that did not permit him to enter; he rushed back to me in utter dismay and dragged me away without giving me time to collect myself; and we were at Beaulieu when he told me the reason for his retreat. Had I known it, I would not have budged from your house, so as to clear up this affair to your advantage; but returning to your house after having left it would no longer have proved anything. Now, whether Stanislas saw wrongly or whether he is right, *he must be wrong*. Dear Naïs, do not let a fool gamble with your life, your honor, your future; silence him at once. You know my position here? Although I need everyone here, I am entirely devoted to you. Dispose of a life that belongs to you. Although you have rejected my suit, my heart will always be yours, and on every occasion I shall prove to you how much I love you. Yes, I shall watch over you like a faithful servant, without hope of reward, solely for the pleasure I find in serving you, even without your knowledge. This morning I told everyone that I had been at the drawing-room door and had seen nothing. If you are asked who informed you of the things being said about you, make use of me. I should be very proud to be your acknowledged defender; but, between ourselves, Monsieur de Bargeton is the only man who can demand satisfaction from Stanislas... Even if that little Rubempré had committed some folly, a woman’s honor cannot be left at the mercy of the first scatterbrain who throws himself at her feet. That is what I said.” Naïs thanked du Châtelet with an inclination of her head and remained thoughtful. She was weary, to the point of disgust, of provincial life. At du Châtelet’s first words, her thoughts had turned to Paris. Madame de Bargeton’s silence placed her learned admirer in an awkward position. “Dispose of me,” he said. “I repeat it.” “Thank you,” she replied. “What do you intend to do?” “I shall see.” A long silence. “Do you love that little Rubempré so much, then?” She let a superb smile escape her and folded her arms as she looked at the curtains of her boudoir. Du Châtelet left without having been able to decipher that proud woman’s heart. When Lucien and the four faithful old gentlemen who had come to play their game, unmoved by this dubious gossip, had departed, Madame de Bargeton detained her husband, who was preparing to go to bed and was opening his mouth to wish his wife good night. “Come over here, my dear, I have something to say to you,” she said with a kind of solemnity. Monsieur de Bargeton followed his wife into the boudoir. “Monsieur,” she said to him, “perhaps I was wrong to put into my protective attentions toward Monsieur de Rubempré an ardor as badly misunderstood by the foolish people of this town as by the young man himself. This morning, Lucien threw himself at my feet, there, and declared his love for me. Stanislas entered at the very moment when I was raising the child to his feet. In contempt of the duties that courtesy imposes upon a gentleman toward a woman in every kind of circumstance, he has claimed to have surprised me in a compromising situation with this boy, whom I was then treating as he deserves. If that thoughtless young man knew the slanders to which his folly has given rise, I know him: he would go and insult Stanislas and force him to fight. That action would be tantamount to a public avowal of his love. I do not need to tell you that your wife is pure; but you will understand that there would be something dishonorable for both you and me if Monsieur de Rubempré were the one to defend her. Go to Stanislas’s at once and formally demand satisfaction for the insulting remarks he has made about me; bear in mind that you must not allow the matter to be settled unless he retracts them in the presence of numerous and important witnesses. In that way you will win the esteem of all decent people; you will conduct yourself as a man of intelligence, as a gentleman, and you will earn a claim to my esteem. I am going to send Gentil on horseback to l’Escarbas; my father must be your second. Despite his age, I know he is a man capable of trampling underfoot that little puppet who is blackening the reputation of a Nègrepelisse. You have the choice of weapons; fight with pistols—you are a marvelous shot.”
