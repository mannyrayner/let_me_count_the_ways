How the scores were assigned

Adolfo’s retrospective “ti ho amata davvero, onestamente” (“I truly, honestly loved you”) explicitly presents his past love for Loredana as true. It makes no present relational undertaking, and its force is controlled avowal used within an accusation rather than expressive or reflex-like discharge.

This weaponized and possibly self-conflicted use affects the interpersonal meaning but does not alter the core utterance’s dominant truth-conditional force.

English translation

AI-generated analytical aid, supplied to the annotator; scope: wide context.

—Even if it is false, it matters little, because when everyone thinks the same way, it is as though it were true. Do you understand me? If someone is accused of being a thief, in order to go about with him and remain his friend, it is not enough to believe and even to know that he is honest; it takes a courage that I do not have, because other people believe he is a thief, and I cannot be the friend of a thief.... All appearances are against you, and all Venice speaks of you as a dangerous little hussy; and what can I do about it?... Besides, there must be something to it; it cannot all have been invented.... But even if there is nothing, absolutely nothing true in it, I have my family reproaching me for loving you and following you, I have my friends laughing, I have the director of the Bank, who does not want his employees associating with bad women; and how am I to persuade all these people that you are not a bad woman, after a scandal that has been talked about for so many days and in such detail in every corner of the city?... It may not be your fault, let us grant that, but you are dishonored, there it is; and your protests are lost in the din, and by now, whatever good and beautiful thing you may do, it will be of no use to you.... He seized the mug of beer, raised it to his lips, and did not put it back on the saucer until he had emptied it. —But then,—said Loredana with a shiver of terror,—does being innocent mean nothing? —It means.... it means.... How should I know what it means?—replied Adolfo, who did not perceive his own cruelty, himself astounded by the soundness of his facile arguments.—What matters in the world is to be believed, rightly or wrongly; even miracles are of no use if no one believes in them.... And no one believes in your honesty.... It would be better for you to be dishonest, truly dishonest, and for everyone to praise your virtue.... A fine ocher-colored background, boldly laid in with vigorous strokes, framed in the sketchbook the little head of the young woman with golden glints in her hair; an effect of light and shade that the artist compared with the original, moving his head to right and left and holding the drawing at a distance. —If that idiot does not stop tormenting her,—he muttered under his breath,—I’ll make a caricature of him! But Adolfo did not stop, exalted by the voluptuous pleasure of torturing the woman who had always been victorious over him, driven by the need to trample upon and destroy his love, darkly satisfied to see his one-time fiancée reduced to defenselessness, drunk with ferocity against her and against himself.... —And besides, why discuss your innocence? I do not believe in it, come now!... Is it possible that an entire city would turn against a woman, a girl, like this, merely on a whim?... You will tell me that those who speak ill of you gain nothing by it!... Why do they not speak ill of so many others?... You see, when people warn me that one must be wary of gossip, I laugh; gossip is spread about those who deserve it; nothing has ever been said about me, for instance?... It would be better not to speak of your innocence, which, besides, even if it existed, would not be worth a damn by now.... What have you set against the specific and precise accusations? That it is all false! But I expected that; you are hardly going to tell me about your love affairs—to me, who truly, honestly loved you.... And you were so afraid of my judgment that you stopped me in the street and asked whether I knew.... There is another proof.... And then, I must add.... Loredana rose slowly. —Thank you,—she interrupted in an unsteady voice.—You have told me some very useful things.... She made to leave and staggered.... —Do not move,—she added, leaning against a corner of the table.—I want to go out alone.... After a few uncertain steps, while Adolfo watched her with lifeless eyes, Loredana regained her strength, walked on resolutely, and passed close to the artist, who, looking back and forth between the pastel and the young woman, felt that he loved them both.... Adolfo remained motionless, slumped on his bench; suddenly remorse invaded his soul, leaving him open-mouthed, with an expression of blank bewilderment. Loredana felt the urge to snatch from the artist’s hands the sketchbook in which she knew she had been portrayed and strike him in the face with it. She had to close her eyes to master herself. She went out into the fog; the fog had descended suddenly, with a faint smell of burning, broken here and there by the reddish halos of the gas flames. And the young woman set off again toward the Zattere, toward Flopi, the one and the other seeming lost in that infinite, dense, acrid expanse. —What am I bringing him?—Loredana asked herself. She was bringing him her body, which the crowd said had been mauled by others, and dishonor. Certainly he was deluding himself, Filippo; no one had hurled all the accusations in his face, and he did not know how much his mistress was despised; she was bringing ridicule into his home like a poisoned fluid. Adolfo Gianella’s words were the echo of that wisdom found on the ground, among the spittle and greasy papers, which is called public opinion. He spoke truly: what matters is not to be, but to appear; when a slander is repeated by everyone, it has the force of truth: evil is what is seen, not what is committed. Aphorisms that kill; stubborn bestiality; the majority’s tyranny, against which there is no appeal.... But life proceeds on this chariot of death, and every day someone falls beneath its wheels because of one mistaken gesture. Loredana blushed for herself; having drunk for long hours from the stream of mud, she felt as though her mouth were full of it, her body splattered, her hands macerated. How could she allow herself to be embraced and kissed by Filippo, who would notice on her face the traces of the sufferings she had endured, a furrow on her brow, a bruise beneath her eyes? To kiss and love each other amid the audience’s jeers? To tremble beneath sardonic gazes? To live beside a man who, unless he quickly and decisively broke away from her, would become ridiculous? The crowd had fallen upon her love and torn it to pieces. Safe and strong protection, tender confidences and fleeting moments of joy, secret tenderness and impetuous outbursts of passion, long oblivion of every mortal thing, dreamed-of journeys, the little house on the Zattere, the beautiful sun of Sirmione—all drowned in the fog forever! She wanted to run home and say to Filippo: —You had me when I was still a child, and through your love you made me a woman. Take me; love me one last time; extinguish in your arms this life that is yours, and do not let others slowly kill, out of hatred, the woman who wants to die for you.
