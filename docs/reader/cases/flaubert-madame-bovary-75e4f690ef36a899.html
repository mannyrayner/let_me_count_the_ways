Emma is strongly affected because such language is new to her. After the hunt, he had thought that it was too late; then he reasoned as follows: “But if she loved me from the very first day, her impatience to see me again must make her love me more. So let us keep it up!” And he realized that his calculation had been correct when, upon entering the room, he saw Emma turn pale. She was alone. Daylight was fading. The little muslin curtains along the windowpanes deepened the twilight, and the gilding of the barometer, struck by a ray of sunlight, spread gleams in the mirror between the jagged outlines of the coral. Rodolphe remained standing, and Emma barely answered his first polite remarks. “As for me,” he said, “I had business to attend to. I was ill.” “Seriously?” she cried. “Well, no!” said Rodolphe, sitting down beside her on a stool. “The truth is, I did not want to come back.” “Why?” “Can’t you guess?” He looked at her once more, but with such intensity that she lowered her head, blushing. He continued: “Emma...” “Sir!” she said, drawing slightly away. “Ah! You see,” he replied in a melancholy voice, “that I was right to wish not to return; for that name, that name which fills my soul and which escaped my lips, you forbid me to use! Madame Bovary!... Why, everyone calls you that!... Besides, it is not your name; it is another man’s name!” He repeated: “Another man’s!” And he hid his face in his hands. “Yes, I think of you continually!... The memory of you drives me to despair! Ah, forgive me!... I shall leave you... Farewell!... I shall go far away..., so far that you will never hear of me again!... And yet..., today..., I do not know what force drove me toward you once more! For one does not struggle against heaven, one cannot resist the smile of angels! One lets oneself be carried away by what is beautiful, charming, adorable!” It was the first time Emma had heard such things said to her; and her pride, like someone relaxing in a steam bath, stretched languidly and fully in the warmth of this language. “But if I did not come,” he continued, “if I could not see you, ah! at least I gazed long upon everything around you. At night, every night, I would rise again, come as far as here, look at your house, the roof shining beneath the moon, the trees in the garden swaying by your window, and a little lamp, a gleam, shining through the panes in the darkness. Ah! Little did you know that there, so near and so far away, was a poor wretch...” She turned toward him with a sob. “Oh! You are kind!” she said. “No, I love you, that is all! You do not doubt it! Tell me so; one word! Just one word!” And Rodolphe, imperceptibly, let himself slide from the stool to the floor; but the clatter of wooden shoes was heard in the kitchen, and he noticed that the door to the room was not closed. “How charitable it would be of you,” he continued, rising again, “to gratify a whim of mine!” It was to visit her house; he wanted to become acquainted with it; and, Madame Bovary seeing no objection, they were both getting up when Charles came in. “Good day, doctor,” Rodolphe said to him. The physician, flattered by this unexpected title, poured forth obsequious courtesies, and the other man took advantage of them to recover his composure a little. “Madame was speaking to me,” he then said, “about her health...” Charles interrupted him: he did indeed have a thousand worries; his wife’s shortness of breath was returning.
