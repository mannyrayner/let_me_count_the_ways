Julien was already cold and haughty. He gave thanks, but in very vague terms that committed him to nothing. Could it really be possible, he said to himself, that I am the natural son of some great lord exiled to our mountains by the terrible Napoleon? At every moment, this idea seemed less improbable to him... My hatred for my father would be proof of it... I would no longer be a monster! A few days after this monologue, the Fifteenth Hussar Regiment, one of the most brilliant in the army, was drawn up in battle formation on the parade ground at Strasbourg. Monsieur the Chevalier de La Vernaye rode the finest horse in Alsace, which had cost him six thousand francs. He had been commissioned as a lieutenant without ever having been a second lieutenant except on the rolls of a regiment of which he had never heard. His impassive manner, his stern and almost malicious eyes, his pallor, and his unalterable composure began to establish his reputation from the very first day. Soon afterward, his perfect and measured courtesy, and his skill with pistols and weapons, which he made known without too much affectation, discouraged any thought of joking aloud at his expense. After five or six days of hesitation, the prevailing opinion in the regiment declared itself in his favor. There is everything in this young man, the old, mocking officers said, except youth. From Strasbourg, Julien wrote to M. Chélan, the former curé of Verrières, who was now approaching the utmost limits of extreme old age. * * * * * “You will have learned, with a joy of which I have no doubt, of the events that have led my family to enrich me. Here are five hundred francs, which I beg you to distribute quietly, without any mention of my name, to the unfortunate, poor now as I once was, whom you no doubt assist as you once assisted me.” * * * * * Julien was intoxicated with ambition and not with vanity; nevertheless, he devoted a great deal of his attention to outward appearance. His horses, his uniforms, and his servants’ liveries were maintained with a correctness that would have done honor to the punctuality of a great English lord. Having been a lieutenant, by favor, for barely two days, he was already calculating that, in order to be commander in chief by the age of thirty at the latest, like all the great generals, he had to be more than a lieutenant at twenty-three. He thought only of glory and of his son. It was in the midst of the transports of the most unbridled ambition that he was surprised by a young footman from the Hôtel de La Mole, who arrived as a courier. * * * * * “Everything is lost,” Mathilde wrote to him, “come as quickly as possible, sacrifice everything, desert if necessary.
