How the scores were assigned

The original “Je vous aime” presents Pierre as loving Hélène and therefore has strong truth-conditional avowal force even though he now judges it false. It also carries substantial performative force because, in his recollection, saying it helped bind him to her and led into marriage. Nothing supports expressive/reflexive discharge: the words are explicitly remembered as not coming from him and as insincere.

Narrative situation

The original annotator’s interpretation of the supplied context.

Pierre retrospectively condemns the declaration as “a lie and worse than a lie.” He says he married Hélène without loving her, deceived both her and himself, and had already felt that he had no right to utter the words. Their falsity does not diminish their T mode; instead, it establishes insincerity and self-deception. His association of the declaration with binding himself to her and with the disastrous marriage supports P, while the description of the words as alien to his genuine feelings strongly opposes E.

“What happened, then?” he asked himself. “I killed my wife’s lover—yes, killed my wife’s lover. Yes, that happened. Why? How did I come to this?” “Because you married her,” answered the inner voice. “But what am I guilty of?” he asked. “Of marrying her without loving her, of deceiving both yourself and her”—and he vividly pictured that moment after dinner at Prince Vasily’s when he had uttered those words that would not come from him: “I love you.” “It all came from that! Even then I felt,” he thought, “I felt then that it was not right, that I had no right to do it. And so it turned out.” He remembered the honeymoon and blushed at the recollection. Especially vivid, insulting, and shameful to him was the memory of how once, soon after his marriage, in the twelfth hour of the day, wearing a silk dressing gown, he had come from the bedroom into his study and there found the chief steward, who bowed respectfully, looked at Pierre’s face and at his dressing gown, and smiled slightly, as though expressing with that smile respectful sympathy for his employer’s happiness. “And how many times I was proud of her, proud of her majestic beauty, of her social tact,” he thought; “proud of that house of mine in which she received all Petersburg, proud of her unapproachability and beauty. So that is what I was proud of! At the time I thought I did not understand her. How often, when pondering her character, I told myself that I was to blame for not understanding her, for not understanding that perpetual calm, contentment, and absence of all passions and desires, while the whole answer lay in that terrible phrase: that she was a depraved woman. I told myself that terrible phrase, and everything became clear! “Anatole used to go to her to borrow money from her and kiss her bare shoulders. She did not give him money, but she allowed him to kiss her. Her father, as a joke, tried to arouse her jealousy; with a calm smile she said she was not so stupid as to be jealous: let him do whatever he wants, she said of me. I once asked her whether she felt any signs of pregnancy. She laughed contemptuously and said that she was not a fool who wanted to have children, and that she would have no children by me.” Then he remembered the coarseness and clarity of her thoughts and the vulgarity of the expressions characteristic of her, despite her upbringing in the highest aristocratic circle. “I’m not some sort of fool... go and try it yourself... go take a walk,” she would say. Often, looking at her success in the eyes of old and young men and women, Pierre could not understand why he did not love her. “Why, I never loved her,” Pierre told himself; “I knew she was a depraved woman,” he repeated to himself, but had not dared admit it. “And now Dolokhov—there he is, sitting in the snow and forcing a smile, and perhaps dying, responding to my remorse with some sort of feigned bravado!” Pierre was one of those people who, despite their outward, so-called weakness of character, do not seek a confidant for their grief. He worked through his grief alone within himself. “She is to blame for everything, for everything—she alone,” he told himself; “but what of it? Why did I bind myself to her, why did I say to her, ‘I love you,’ which was a lie and worse than a lie?” he told himself. “I am guilty and must bear... What? The disgrace of my name, the unhappiness of my life? Ah, it is all nonsense,” he thought, “the disgrace of one’s name and honor—it is all conventional, all independent of me. “Louis XVI was executed because they said he was dishonorable and a criminal,” it occurred to Pierre, “and they were right from their point of view, just as those who died martyrs’ deaths for him and numbered him among the saints were also right. Then Robespierre was executed for being a despot. Who is right, who is guilty? No one. But you are alive, so live: tomorrow you will die, as I might have died an hour ago. And is it worth suffering when one has only a second left to live in comparison with eternity?” But at the very moment when he considered himself calmed by reasoning of that kind, he would suddenly picture her in those moments when he had most strongly displayed his insincere love for her, and he would feel a rush of blood to his heart and have to get up again, move about, and break and tear whatever came to hand. “Why did I say to her, ‘I love you’?” he kept repeating to himself. At night he summoned his valet and ordered him to pack for the journey to Petersburg. He could not remain under the same roof with her. He could not imagine how he would speak to her now. He decided that the next day he would leave and leave her a letter in which he would announce his intention of separating from her forever. He woke and for a long time looked around in fright, unable to understand where he was. “The Countess ordered me to ask whether Your Excellency is at home,” said the valet. With her imperturbable composure, she did not begin speaking in the valet’s presence. She knew about the duel and had come to speak about it. She waited until the valet had set out the coffee and left. Pierre looked timidly at her through his spectacles, and, as a hare surrounded by dogs, flattening its ears, continues to lie in sight of its enemies, so he tried to continue reading; but he felt that this was senseless and impossible and again glanced timidly at her.
