How the scores were assigned

Armand presents his love for Marguerite as an established fact, reminding her of it to counter her fear that his father may persuade him to abandon her. Narrative situation

The original annotator’s interpretation of the supplied context.

Marguerite fears that Armand's father will use her past to persuade Armand to leave her. Armand answers by invoking her existing knowledge of his love, chiefly as reassurance and as a reason she should discount that possibility. His surrounding refusal to abandon her and his plan to persuade his father strengthen the sincerity and constancy attributed to the avowal, while the later plot pressure does not alter its core force here.

—My notary. Would an honest man have done such a thing without warning me? Well, it is to prevent you from ruining yourself over a girl that I have come to Paris. When your mother died, she left you enough to live honorably, not enough to lavish gifts on your mistresses. —I swear to you, Father, Marguerite knew nothing of this gift. —Then why were you making it? —Because Marguerite, this woman whom you slander and whom you want me to abandon, is sacrificing everything she possesses in order to live with me. —And you accept this sacrifice? What kind of man are you, sir, to allow a Mademoiselle Marguerite to sacrifice anything for you? Come now, that is enough. You will leave this woman. A moment ago I was asking you; now I am ordering you. I will have no such filth in my family. Pack your bags and prepare to come with me. —Forgive me, Father, I then said, but I will not leave. —Because?... —Because I have already reached the age when one no longer obeys an order. My father turned pale at this reply. —Very well, sir, he resumed; I know what remains for me to do. He rang. Joseph appeared. —Have my bags taken to the Hôtel de Paris, he said to my servant. At the same time, he went into his room, where he finished dressing. When he reappeared, I went to meet him. —Will you promise me, Father, I said to him, not to do anything that might cause Marguerite pain? My father stopped, looked at me disdainfully, and merely replied: —I believe you are mad. After which he went out, violently slamming the door behind him. I went downstairs in my turn, took a cabriolet, and left for Bougival. Marguerite was waiting for me at the window. Chapter XXI —At last! she exclaimed, throwing her arms around my neck. Here you are! How pale you are! Then I told her about my confrontation with my father. —Oh, my God! I suspected as much, she said. When Joseph came to tell us of your father’s arrival, I shuddered as though at news of some calamity. Poor darling! And it is I who am causing you all this sorrow. Perhaps you would do better to leave me than to fall out with your father. And yet I have done nothing to him. We are living very quietly, and we are going to live more quietly still. He knows very well that you must have a mistress, and he ought to be happy that it is I, since I love you and desire no more than your position allows.
