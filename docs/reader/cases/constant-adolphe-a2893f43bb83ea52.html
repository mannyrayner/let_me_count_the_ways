How the scores were assigned

The utterance primarily presents Adolphe's love as presently true, so it has strong truth-conditional force even though the narration strongly suggests that the avowal is false or self-deceptive. In immediately reversing an attempted separation and asking Ellénore to return to him, it also has moderate force as a renewal of their relationship, though it contains no explicit promise. His crying out in an emotional crisis indicates intense delivery but does not independently establish expressive/reflexive force.

Ambiguity noted by the annotator

The extent of performative force is uncertain: the avowal effectively renews the relationship, but it may principally be an expedient reassurance rather than a deliberate undertaking. Adolphe may retain affection despite lacking the passionate love he claims.

Narrative situation

The original annotator’s interpretation of the supplied context.

Adolphe has just told Ellénore that he no longer feels passionate love, intending to free himself and induce her to accept the count's offer. After she collapses, he reverses himself and claims both that he loves her tenderly and that his prior denial was a benevolent deception. Ellénore believes the avowal and renews her sacrifice, while the narrator undercuts it by saying that she mistakes her own love for a mutual one. Thus the utterance operates as a likely deceptive or self-deceptive avowal and relational reassurance whose practical effect is to bind Adolphe more deeply.

One day, I saw Ellénore agitated and trying to conceal from me an idea that preoccupied her. After much entreaty, she made me promise that I would not oppose the resolution she had taken, and confessed to me that M. de P** had written to her: he had won his lawsuit; he remembered with gratitude the services she had rendered him, and their relationship of ten years. He offered her half his fortune, not in order to reunite with her, which was no longer possible, but on condition that she leave the ungrateful and treacherous man who had separated them. “I have replied,” she told me, “and you can well guess that I refused.” I guessed it only too well. I was touched, but in despair at the new sacrifice Ellénore was making for me. Nevertheless, I dared raise no objection: my attempts in that direction had always been so fruitless! I withdrew to reflect on the course I had to take. It was clear to me that our bonds had to be broken. They were painful to me; they were becoming harmful to her; I was the sole obstacle preventing her from recovering a suitable position and the consideration which, in society, sooner or later follows opulence; I was the only barrier between her and her children: I no longer had any excuse in my own eyes. To yield to her in these circumstances was no longer generosity, but culpable weakness. I had promised my father that I would become free again as soon as I was no longer necessary to Ellénore. At last it was time to embark upon a career, to begin an active life, to acquire some claim to the esteem of men, to make noble use of my faculties. I returned to Ellénore, believing myself unshakable in my resolve to force her not to reject the Count de P**’s offers and, if necessary, to tell her that I no longer loved her. “Dear friend,” I said to her, “one struggles for a time against one’s destiny, but in the end one always yields. The laws of society are stronger than the wills of men; the most imperious feelings break against the fatality of circumstances. In vain does one persist in consulting only one’s heart; sooner or later one is condemned to listen to reason. I cannot keep you any longer in a position equally unworthy of you and of me; I cannot do so either for your sake or for my own.” As I spoke without looking at Ellénore, I felt my ideas becoming vaguer and my resolve weakening. I tried to summon my strength again, and continued in a hurried voice: “I shall always be your friend; I shall always feel the deepest affection for you. The two years of our relationship will not be erased from my memory; they will forever remain the finest period of my life. But love—that transport of the senses, that involuntary intoxication, that oblivion of all interests, of all duties—Ellénore, I no longer feel it.” I waited a long time for her reply without raising my eyes to her. When at last I looked at her, she was motionless; she gazed at everything around her as though she recognized none of it; I took her hand: I found it cold. She pushed me away. “What do you want of me?” she said. “Am I not alone, alone in the universe, alone without a single being who understands me? What more have you to say to me? Have you not told me everything? Is it not all over, over beyond recall? Leave me, go away; is that not what you desire?” She tried to move away; she staggered; I tried to hold her, and she fell unconscious at my feet; I lifted her up, embraced her, restored her to consciousness. “Ellénore,” I cried, “come back to yourself, come back to me; I love you with love, with the tenderest love; I deceived you so that you might be freer in your choice.” Credulities of the heart, you are inexplicable! These simple words, contradicted by so many words that had preceded them, restored Ellénore to life and trust; she made me repeat them several times: she seemed to breathe avidly. She believed me: she became intoxicated with her love, which she mistook for ours; she confirmed her reply to the Count de P**, and I found myself more deeply committed than ever. Three months later, a new possibility of change appeared in Ellénore’s situation. One of those vicissitudes common in republics convulsed by factions recalled her father to Poland and restored him to his estates. Although he scarcely knew his daughter, whom her mother had taken to France at the age of three, he wished to have her settle near him. Only vague reports of Ellénore’s adventures had reached him in Russia, where he had lived throughout his exile. Ellénore was his only child: he feared isolation, and wanted to be cared for; he sought only to discover his daughter’s dwelling, and as soon as he learned where it was, he urged her to come and join him. She could feel no real attachment to a father whom she did not remember ever having seen. Nevertheless, she felt that it was her duty to obey; in this way she would secure a great fortune for her children, and would herself rise again to the rank of which her misfortunes and her conduct had deprived her; but she declared to me positively that she would go to Poland only if I accompanied her. “I am no longer,” she told me, “at the age when the soul opens itself to new impressions. My father is a stranger to me. If I remain here, others will hasten to surround him; he will be just as happy. My children will have M. de P**’s fortune. I know very well that I shall be generally blamed; I shall be regarded as an ungrateful daughter and an unfeeling mother: but I have suffered too much; I am no longer young enough for the opinion of the world to have great power over me. If there is something harsh in my resolution, it is you, Adolphe, whom you must blame. If I could deceive myself about you, I might perhaps consent to an absence whose bitterness would be lessened by the prospect of a sweet and lasting reunion; but you would like nothing better than to imagine me two hundred leagues away from you, contented and tranquil, amid my family and opulence. You would write me reasonable letters on that subject, which I can already foresee; they would tear my heart apart; I do not wish to expose myself to that. I do not have the consolation of telling myself that, by the sacrifice of my entire life, I have succeeded in inspiring in you the feeling I deserved; but after all, you accepted that sacrifice. I already suffer enough from the barrenness of your manner and the coldness of our relations; I endure these sufferings that you inflict on me; I do not wish to brave any voluntarily.” There was in Ellénore’s voice and tone something harsh and violent that bespoke a firm determination rather than a deep or touching emotion. For some time she had grown angry in advance whenever she asked something of me, as though I had already refused it. She disposed of my actions, but she knew that my judgment repudiated them. She would have liked to penetrate the innermost sanctuary of my thoughts in order to shatter there a mute opposition that incensed her against me. I spoke to her of my situation, of my father’s wish, of my own desire; I pleaded, I became heated. Ellénore remained unshakable. I tried to reawaken her generosity, as though love were not the most selfish of all feelings and consequently, when wounded, the least generous. By a strange effort, I tried to move her to pity for the unhappiness I experienced in remaining near her; I succeeded only in exasperating her. I promised to go and see her in Poland; but in my promises, devoid of warmth and unreserve, she saw only my impatience to leave her.
