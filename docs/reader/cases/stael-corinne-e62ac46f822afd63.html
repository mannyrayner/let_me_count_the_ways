Corinne, oppressed by this silence, sat down, covering her face with her hands. Lord Nelvil paced briskly about the room for some time; then he approached Corinne and was on the point of voicing his grievance and yielding to what he felt; but a movement of pride, utterly invincible in his character, repressed his tenderness, and he returned to the paintings as though waiting for Corinne to finish showing them to him. She hoped for much from the effect of the last of all; and, making an effort in her turn to appear calm, she rose and said: “Milord, I still have three landscapes left to show you; two allude to certain interesting ideas: I do not much like rural scenes, which are insipid in painting, like idylls, when they make no allusion to Fable or history. What is best, it seems to me, in this genre, is the manner of Salvator Rosa, who represents, as you see in this painting, a rock, torrents, and trees, without a single living being, without even the flight of a bird to recall the idea of life. The absence of man amid nature arouses profound reflections. What would this earth be, thus forsaken? A work without a purpose, and yet a work still so beautiful, whose mysterious impression would be addressed only to the Divinity! “Finally, here are the two paintings in which, in my opinion, history and poetry are happily united with landscape. One represents the moment when Cincinnatus is invited by the consuls to leave his plough in order to command the Roman armies. It is all the luxuriance of the South that you will see in this landscape: its abundant vegetation, its burning sky, that cheerful air of all nature which is found even in the countenance of the plants. And this other painting, which contrasts with it, is the son of Cairbar asleep upon his father’s tomb. For three days and three nights he has been awaiting the bard who is to pay honors to the memory of the dead. During those Carnival days, when exaggeration and caricature are permitted, the most comical scenes take place between the masked revelers. It has grace above all in the mouths of children. The innocence of that age and the language’s natural mischievousness form a very piquant contrast. Finally, one might say that it is a language that goes by itself, expresses things without anyone’s intervention, and almost always appears to have more wit than the person who speaks it.
