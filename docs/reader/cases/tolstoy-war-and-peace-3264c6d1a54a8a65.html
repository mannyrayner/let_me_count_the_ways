“Is that you, Clément?” he said. “Where the devil...” But he did not finish; recognizing his mistake and frowning slightly, he greeted Dolokhov as a stranger and asked how he could be of service. Dolokhov explained that he and his comrade were catching up with their regiment and, addressing everyone in general, asked whether the officers knew anything about the 6th Regiment. No one knew anything; and it seemed to Petya that the officers began looking him and Dolokhov over with hostility and suspicion. For several seconds everyone was silent. “If you are counting on the evening soup, you have come too late,” said a voice from beyond the fire, with restrained laughter. Dolokhov replied that they had eaten their fill and that they had to travel on that very night. He handed the horses over to the soldier who was stirring the pot and squatted down by the fire beside the officer with the long neck. Without taking his eyes off Dolokhov, this officer looked at him and asked him once again what regiment he belonged to. Dolokhov did not answer, as though he had not heard the question, and, lighting a French pipe that he had taken from his pocket, asked the officers how safe the road ahead of them was from the Cossacks. “The brigands are everywhere,” replied the officer beyond the fire. Dolokhov said that the Cossacks were dangerous only to stragglers such as him and his comrade, but that the Cossacks probably did not dare attack large detachments, he added questioningly. No one answered. “Well, now he will leave,” Petya thought every moment as he stood before the fire and listened to their conversation. But Dolokhov again took up the conversation that had died away and began asking directly how many men they had in the battalion, how many battalions there were, and how many prisoners. Asking about the Russian prisoners who were with their detachment, Dolokhov said: “It is a nasty business, dragging these corpses along behind one. It would be better to shoot this rabble,” and he laughed loudly with such a strange laugh that it seemed to Petya that the French would discover the deception at once, and he involuntarily stepped back from the fire. No one responded to Dolokhov’s words or laughter, and a French officer who could not be seen—he was lying wrapped in his greatcoat—raised himself and whispered something to his comrade. Dolokhov got up and called to the soldier with the horses. “Will they bring the horses or not?” thought Petya, involuntarily moving closer to Dolokhov. The horses were brought. “Good day, gentlemen,” said Dolokhov. Petya wanted to say “good evening” but could not finish the words. The officers were whispering something among themselves. Dolokhov took a long time mounting his horse, which would not stand still; then he rode out of the gate at a walk. Petya rode beside him, wanting to look back, yet not daring to, to see whether or not the French were running after them. On reaching the road, Dolokhov did not ride back toward the fields, but along the village. At one point he stopped and listened. “Do you hear?” he said. Petya recognized the sound of Russian voices and saw the dark figures of Russian prisoners by the fires. Descending toward the bridge, Petya and Dolokhov rode past the sentry, who, without saying a word, paced gloomily over the bridge, and rode out into the hollow where the Cossacks were waiting. “Well, goodbye now.
