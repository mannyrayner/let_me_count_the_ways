How the scores were assigned

The embedded negative cessative proposition, “je ne vous aimais plus” (“I no longer loved you”), represents the cessation of the letter-writer’s loving state as something the addressee had become convinced of. Its force is wholly truth-conditional under a reported-belief frame. It neither undertakes a commitment nor functions as an affective verbal discharge.

Ambiguity noted by the annotator

The clause is embedded under the writer’s report of the addressee’s conviction, so it does not straightforwardly avow the writer’s actual state. Whether that conviction was fully true is distinct from its strongly truth-conditional mode.

Narrative situation

The original annotator’s interpretation of the supplied context.

The writer deliberately made her reassurances appear forced so that the addressee would infer that her feelings had changed. The highlighted clause reports the resulting conviction and participates in a calculated strategy of concealment and revenge. Those deceptive and manipulative circumstances concern the proposition’s truth and use, not its core semantic mode; the adjacent resolution never to see him again also does not turn this embedded state-description into a performative undertaking.

English translation

AI-generated analytical aid, supplied to the annotator; scope: wide context.

“I have loved you too much to let you believe that the change you perceive in me is an effect of my fickleness; I want you to know that your infidelity is the cause of it. You are very surprised that I speak to you of your infidelity; you had concealed it from me with so much skill, and I took such care to conceal from you that I knew of it, that you have reason to be astonished that it is known to me. I am surprised myself that I was able to keep you from seeing any sign of it. Never has there been sorrow equal to mine. I believed that you had a violent passion for me; I no longer concealed from you the passion I had for you, and at the very time when I was letting you see it in its entirety, I learned that you were deceiving me, that you loved another woman, and that, to all appearances, you were sacrificing me to this new mistress. I learned it on the day of the ring-tilting; that is why I did not go. I pretended to be ill in order to conceal the disorder of my mind; but I did indeed become ill, and my body could not withstand so violent an agitation. When I began to recover, I continued to pretend that I was very ill, in order to have a pretext for neither seeing you nor writing to you. I wanted time to decide how I should conduct myself toward you; twenty times I made and abandoned the same resolutions; but at last I found you unworthy of seeing my sorrow, and I resolved not to let you perceive it. I wanted to wound your pride by showing you that my passion was weakening of itself. I believed that in this way I would diminish the value of the sacrifice you were making of it; I did not want you to have the pleasure of showing how much I loved you in order to appear more lovable for it. I resolved to write you cool and languishing letters, in order to instill in the mind of the woman to whom you gave them the belief that you were ceasing to be loved. I did not want her to have the pleasure of learning that I knew she was triumphing over me, nor to increase her triumph through my despair and my reproaches. I thought that I would not punish you enough by breaking with you, and that I would cause you only slight pain if I ceased to love you when you no longer loved me. I found that you had to love me in order to feel the pain of not being loved, which I was experiencing so cruelly. I believed that if anything could rekindle the feelings you had had for me, it was to show you that mine had changed; but to show it to you while pretending to conceal it from you, and as though I had not had the strength to confess it to you. I settled upon this resolution; but how difficult it was for me to make, and how impossible it seemed to me to carry out when I saw you again! A hundred times I was on the verge of bursting forth in reproaches and tears; the state my health was still in helped me disguise from you my distress and affliction. I was sustained afterward by the pleasure of dissembling with you, as you dissembled with me; nevertheless, I did myself such great violence in telling you and writing to you that I loved you that you saw sooner than I had intended to let you see that my feelings had changed. You were wounded by it; you complained of it. I tried to reassure you; but it was in so forced a manner that you were only the more convinced that I no longer loved you. At last, I did everything I had intended to do. The capriciousness of your heart made you return to me as you saw me drawing away from you. I have enjoyed all the pleasure that vengeance can give; it has seemed to me that you loved me more than you had ever done, and I have shown you that I no longer loved you. I have had reason to believe that you had entirely abandoned the woman for whom you had left me. I have also had reasons to be persuaded that you had never spoken to her of me; but your return and your discretion have not been able to repair your fickleness. Your heart has been divided between me and another woman; you have deceived me. That is enough to deprive me of the pleasure of being loved by you as I believed I deserved to be, and to leave me in the resolution I have made never to see you again, at which you are so surprised.” Madame de Clèves read this letter and reread it several times, without knowing, however, what she had read. She saw only that Monsieur de Nemours did not love her as she had thought, and that he loved other women whom he deceived as he deceived her. What a sight and what knowledge for a person of her disposition, who had a violent passion, who had just given signs of it to a man whom she judged unworthy of it, and to another whom she treated badly for his sake! Never had affliction been so piercing and so keen: it seemed to her that what gave this affliction its bitterness was what had happened that day, and that, if Monsieur de Nemours had had no reason to believe that she loved him, she would not have cared that he had loved another woman. But she was deceiving herself; and this pain that she found so unbearable was jealousy, with all the horrors that may accompany it. She saw from this letter that Monsieur de Nemours had had a love affair for a long time. She found that the woman who had written the letter had wit and merit; she seemed to her worthy of being loved; she found in her more courage than she found in herself, and she envied the strength she had had to conceal her feelings from Monsieur de Nemours. She saw from the end of the letter that this person believed herself loved; she thought that the discretion this prince had shown her, and by which she had been so deeply moved, was perhaps only an effect of the passion he had for this other person, whom he feared to displease. In short, she thought everything that could increase her affliction and her despair. How many times did she turn her thoughts back upon herself! What reflections she made upon the counsel her mother had given her! How greatly she repented of not having persisted in withdrawing from society, despite Monsieur de Clèves, or of not having followed the thought she had had of confessing to him the inclination she had for Monsieur de Nemours! She found that she would have done better to reveal it to a husband whose goodness she knew, and who would have had an interest in concealing it, than to let it be seen by a man who was unworthy of it, who deceived her, who was perhaps sacrificing her, and who thought of being loved by her only out of a feeling of pride and vanity. At last, she found that all the misfortunes that could befall her, and all the extremities to which she might be driven, were less than having let Monsieur de Nemours see that she loved him and knowing that he loved another woman. All that consoled her was the thought that, after acquiring this knowledge, she at least had nothing more to fear from herself, and that she would be entirely cured of the inclination she had for this prince.”
