How the scores were assigned

Marguerite’s “rappelez-vous seulement que je vous aime” (“just remember that I love you”) explicitly presents her love for Armand as an enduring emotional state he should treat as true. It supports her attempt to reassure him and secure his agreement, but the core utterance does not itself substantially undertake a commitment. Its deliberate, reason-giving formulation supplies no independent expressive/reflexive force.

She was pondering! What about? I do not know; as for me, I was looking at her with love and almost with terror, thinking of what I was prepared to suffer for her. —Dost thou know what I was thinking about? —No. —A scheme I have devised. —And what is this scheme? —I cannot confide it to thee yet, but I can tell thee what would result from it. The result would be that, a month from now, I would be free, I would no longer owe anything, and we would go and spend the summer together in the country. —And you cannot tell me by what means? —No, all that is needed is for thee to love me as I love thee, and everything will succeed. —And you alone devised this scheme? —Yes. —And you will carry it out alone? —I alone will have the troubles, Marguerite told me with a smile I shall never forget, but we shall share the profits. I could not help blushing at the word profits; I remembered Manon Lescaut, together with Desgrieux, living on M. de B...'s money. I replied in a somewhat harsh tone as I rose: —You will permit me, my dear Marguerite, to share only in the profits of ventures that I devise and carry out myself. —What does that mean? —It means that I strongly suspect Count de G... of being your partner in this happy scheme, whose burdens and profits alike I decline to share. —You are a child. I thought you loved me; I was mistaken. Very well. At the same time, she rose, opened her piano, and began playing the Invitation to the Waltz again, up to that famous passage in the major key where she always stopped. Was it out of habit, or to remind me of the day we had met? All I know is that with that melody the memories came back to me, and, going over to her, I took her head between my hands and kissed her. —Do you forgive me? I said to her. —You can see that I do, she replied; but take note that we are only on the second day, and already I have something to forgive you for. You keep your promises of blind obedience very poorly. —What can you expect, Marguerite? I love you too much, and I am jealous of the slightest of your thoughts. What you proposed to me just now would drive me mad with joy, but the mystery preceding the execution of this plan makes my heart ache. —Come, let us reason a little, she resumed, taking both my hands and looking at me with a charming smile that I found it impossible to resist; you love me, do you not? And you would be happy to spend three or four months in the country alone with me; I too would be happy in this solitude for two—not only would I be happy in it, but I need it for my health. I cannot leave Paris for so long without putting my affairs in order, and the affairs of a woman like me are always very tangled; well, I have found a way to reconcile everything—my affairs and my love for you; yes, for you, do not laugh, I am mad enough to love you! And now you put on your grand airs and say grand things to me. Child, child three times over, just remember that I love you, and do not concern yourself about anything.—It is agreed, then, is it not? —Whatever you wish is agreed; you know that perfectly well. —Then, before a month has passed, we shall be in some village, walking along the water's edge and drinking milk. It seems strange to you to hear me speak this way, I, Marguerite Gautier; that is because, my friend, when this Paris life, which seems to make me so happy, does not burn me, it bores me, and then I have sudden longings for a calmer existence that would remind me of my childhood. One has always had a childhood, whatever one may have become. Oh! Do not worry, I am not about to tell you that I am the daughter of a retired colonel and that I was brought up at Saint-Denis. I am a poor country girl, and six years ago I did not know how to write my own name. There, you are reassured, are you not? Why are you the first person to whom I have turned to share the joy of the desire that has come over me? No doubt because I recognized that you loved me for my sake and not for your own, whereas the others have never loved me except for theirs. “I have been to the country very often, but never as I would have wished to go there. It is upon you that I am counting for this simple happiness, so do not be unkind, and grant it to me. Tell yourself this: she is not destined to live to old age, and one day I would repent of not having done for her the first thing she asked of me, when it was so easy to do.” What answer could be made to such words, especially with the memory of a first night of love and in expectation of a second? An hour later, I was holding Marguerite in my arms, and had she asked me to commit a crime, I would have obeyed her. At six in the morning I left, and before leaving I said to her: —This evening? She kissed me more tightly, but she did not answer. During the day, I received a letter containing these words: “Dear child, I am feeling a little unwell, and the doctor has ordered me to rest. I shall go to bed early this evening and shall not see you. But, to reward you, I shall expect you tomorrow at noon. I love you.” My first words were: “She is cheating on me!” An icy sweat passed over my brow, for I already loved this woman too much for such a suspicion not to overwhelm me. And yet, with Marguerite, I ought to have expected such an occurrence almost every day, and it had often happened to me with my other mistresses without my being greatly concerned about it. Whence, then, came the power this woman was gaining over my life? Then, since I had the key to her home, I thought of going to see her as usual. In that way I would very quickly learn the truth, and if I found a man there, I would slap his face. In the meantime, I went to the Champs-Élysées. I remained there for four hours. She did not appear. That evening, I went into every theater she habitually attended. She was in none of them. At eleven o'clock, I went to the Rue d'Antin. There was no light in Marguerite's windows. Nevertheless, I rang. The porter asked me where I was going.
