How the scores were assigned

“Je vous aime avec passion” presents Julien as having passionate love for Mme de Rênal, so its core force is a strong avowal and therefore truth-conditional, regardless of sincerity. It does not itself undertake a commitment, and neither the sigh nor the emotional setting shows that the words are a reflex-like affective discharge.

Ambiguity noted by the annotator

Julien’s mixture of calculation, vanity, genuine disturbance, and possible self-deception makes the avowal’s sincerity uncertain. It may pragmatically advance his seduction, but courtship strategy alone does not establish performative force.

Narrative situation

The original annotator’s interpretation of the supplied context.

The avowal is delivered as a reason Julien must leave and is framed as a forbidden fault for a young priest. Its apparent confession is complicated by his preceding decision to make Mme de Rênal his lover out of ambition, resentment, and vanity. Yet the narration also says that his inner state does not fully match his cavalier self-description and that he is deeply troubled, leaving room for feelings he does not understand or honestly acknowledge. These complications affect sincerity and motive, not the utterance’s primarily assertive-avowal force.

Certaine de l'affection de Julien, peut-être sa vertu eût trouvé des forces contre lui. Tremblante de le perdre à jamais, sa passion l'égara jusqu'au point de reprendre la main de Julien que, dans sa distraction, il avait laissée appuyée sur le dossier d'une chaise. Cette action réveilla ce jeune ambitieux: il eût voulu qu'elle eût pour témoins tous ces nobles si fiers qui, à table, lorsqu'il était au bas bout avec les enfants, le regardaient avec un sourire si protecteur. Cette femme ne peut plus me mépriser: dans ce cas, se dit-il, je dois être sensible à sa beauté; je me dois à moi-même d'être son amant! Une telle idée ne lui fût pas venue avant les confidences naïves faites par son ami. La détermination subite qu'il venait de prendre forma une distraction agréable. Il se disait: il faut que j'aie une de ces deux femmes, il s'aperçut qu'il aurait beaucoup mieux aimé faire la cour à Mme Derville; ce n'est pas qu'elle fût plus agréable, mais toujours elle l'avait vu précepteur honoré pour sa science, et non pas ouvrier charpentier, avec une veste de ratine pliée sous le bras, comme il était apparu à Mme de Rênal. C'était précisément comme jeune ouvrier, rougissant jusqu'au blanc des yeux, arrêté à la porte de la maison et n'osant sonner, que Mme de Rênal se le figurait avec le plus de charme. Cette femme, que les bourgeois du pays disaient si hautaine, songeait rarement au rang et la moindre certitude l'emportait de beaucoup dans son esprit sur la promesse de caractère faite par le rang d'un homme. Un charretier qui eût montré de la bravoure eût été plus brave dans son esprit qu'un terrible capitaine de hussards garni de sa moustache et de sa pipe. Elle croyait l'âme de Julien plus noble que celle de tous ses cousins, tous gentilshommes de race et plusieurs d'entre eux titrés. En poursuivant la revue de sa position, Julien vit qu'il ne fallait pas songer à la conquête de Mme Derville, qui s'apercevait probablement du goût que Mme de Rênal montrait pour lui. Forcé de revenir à celle-ci: Que connais-je du caractère de cette femme? se dit Julien. Seulement ceci: avant mon voyage, je lui prenais la main, elle la retirait; aujourd'hui je retire ma main, elle la saisit et la serre. Belle occasion de lui rendre tous les mépris qu'elle a eus pour moi. Dieu sait combien elle a eu d'amants! elle ne se décide peut-être en ma faveur qu'à cause de la facilité des entrevues. Tel est, hélas! le malheur d'une excessive civilisation! A vingt ans, l'éducation d'un jeune homme, s'il a quelque éducation, est à mille lieues du laisser-aller, sans lequel l'amour n'est souvent que le plus ennuyeux des devoirs. Je me dois d'autant plus, continua la petite vanité de Julien, de réussir auprès de cette femme, que si jamais je fais fortune et que quelqu'un me reproche le bas emploi de précepteur, je pourrai faire entendre que l'amour m'avait jeté à cette place. Julien éloigna de nouveau sa main de celle de Mme de Rênal, puis il la reprit en la serrant. Comme on rentrait au salon, vers minuit, Mme de Rênal lui dit à mi-voix: --Vous nous quitterez, vous partirez? Julien répondit en soupirant: --Il faut bien que je parte, car je vous aime avec passion; c'est une faute... et quelle faute pour un jeune prêtre! Mme de Rênal s'appuya sur son bras, et avec tant d'abandon que sa joue sentit la chaleur de celle de Julien. Les nuits de ces deux êtres furent bien différentes. Mme de Rênal était exaltée par les transports de la volupté morale la plus élevée. Une jeune fille coquette qui aime de bonne heure s'accoutume au trouble de l'amour; quand elle arrive à l'âge de la vraie passion, le charme de la nouveauté manque. Comme Mme de Rênal n'avait jamais lu de romans, toutes les nuances de son bonheur étaient neuves pour elle. Aucune triste vérité ne venait la glacer, pas même le spectre de l'avenir. Elle se vit aussi heureuse dans dix ans qu'elle l'était en ce moment. L'idée même de la vertu et de la fidélité jurée à M. de Rênal, qui l'avait agitée quelques jours auparavant, se présenta en vain, on la renvoya comme un hôte importun. Jamais je n'accorderai rien à Julien se dit Mme de Rênal, nous vivrons à l'avenir comme nous vivons depuis un mois. Ce sera un ami. Certain of Julien’s affection, perhaps her virtue would have found the strength to resist him. Trembling at the thought of losing him forever, her passion led her astray to the point of taking Julien’s hand again, which, in his distraction, he had left resting on the back of a chair. This action roused the young man’s ambition: he would have liked all those proud nobles to have witnessed it, those who, at table, when he sat at the lower end with the children, looked at him with such a patronizing smile. This woman can no longer despise me: in that case, he told himself, I must be sensitive to her beauty; I owe it to myself to become her lover! Such an idea would not have occurred to him before the guileless confidences his friend had shared with him. The sudden resolution he had just made provided a pleasant distraction. He told himself: I must have one of these two women; he realized that he would have much preferred to court Mme Derville; not that she was more attractive, but she had always seen him as a tutor honored for his learning, and not as a carpenter’s workman, with a coarse woolen jacket folded under his arm, as he had appeared to Mme de Rênal. It was precisely as a young workman, blushing up to the whites of his eyes, stopped at the door of the house and not daring to ring, that Mme de Rênal pictured him at his most charming. This woman, whom the local bourgeois called so haughty, rarely thought about rank, and the slightest certainty carried far more weight in her mind than the promise of character afforded by a man’s rank. A carter who had shown bravery would, in her mind, have been braver than a formidable captain of hussars adorned with his mustache and his pipe. She believed Julien’s soul nobler than those of all her cousins, all gentlemen of ancient lineage and several of them titled. As he continued reviewing his position, Julien saw that he must not think of conquering Mme Derville, who was probably aware of the liking Mme de Rênal showed for him. Forced to return to the latter: What do I know of this woman’s character? Julien asked himself. Only this: before my journey, I took her hand and she withdrew it; today I withdraw my hand, and she seizes it and presses it. A fine opportunity to repay her for all the contempt she has shown me. God knows how many lovers she has had! Perhaps she decides in my favor only because of the ease with which we can meet. Such, alas, is the misfortune of excessive civilization! At twenty, a young man’s education, if he has any education at all, is a thousand leagues removed from the freedom from constraint without which love is often merely the most tedious of duties. I owe it all the more to myself, Julien’s petty vanity continued, to succeed with this woman, because if I ever make my fortune and someone reproaches me for the lowly employment of tutor, I shall be able to hint that love had cast me into that position. Julien once again drew his hand away from Mme de Rênal’s, then took hers again, pressing it. As they were returning to the drawing room, around midnight, Mme de Rênal said to him in a low voice: “You will leave us, you will go away?” Julien replied with a sigh: “I really must go, for I love you passionately; it is a sin... and what a sin for a young priest!” Mme de Rênal leaned upon his arm, and with such abandon that her cheek felt the warmth of Julien’s. The nights of these two beings were very different. Mme de Rênal was exalted by the transports of the most elevated moral ecstasy. A coquettish young girl who begins loving early grows accustomed to the turmoil of love; when she reaches the age of true passion, the charm of novelty is lacking. As Mme de Rênal had never read novels, every nuance of her happiness was new to her. No sad truth came to chill her, not even the specter of the future. She saw herself as happy ten years hence as she was at that moment. Even the idea of virtue and of the fidelity she had sworn to M. de Rênal, which had troubled her a few days earlier, presented itself in vain; it was sent away like an unwelcome guest. I shall never grant Julien anything, Mme de Rênal told herself; from now on we shall live as we have lived for the past month. He will be a friend. POLIDORI For Julien, Fouqué’s offer had indeed deprived him of all happiness; he could not settle upon any course. Alas! perhaps I lack character; I would have made a bad soldier of Napoleon. At least, he added, my little intrigue with the lady of the house will distract me for a while. Fortunately for him, even in this minor, subordinate incident, the depths of his soul corresponded poorly to his cavalier language. He was afraid of Mme de Rênal because of her very pretty dress. In his eyes, that dress was the vanguard of Paris. His pride would leave nothing to chance or to the inspiration of the moment. On the basis of Fouqué’s confidences and the little he had read about love in his Bible, he devised a highly detailed plan of campaign. Since, without admitting it to himself, he was greatly troubled, he wrote this plan down. The following morning in the drawing room, Mme de Rênal was alone with him for a moment: “Have you no other name but Julien?” she asked him. At this most flattering question, our hero did not know how to reply. This circumstance had not been foreseen in his plan. But for the folly of making a plan, Julien’s lively mind would have served him well; surprise would merely have added to the quickness of his perceptions. He was awkward and exaggerated his awkwardness to himself. Mme de Rênal quickly forgave him for it. She saw in it the effect of a charming innocence. And precisely what she thought lacking in this man, whom everyone considered so gifted, was an air of innocence. “Your little tutor inspires a great deal of mistrust in me,” Mme Derville sometimes told her. “I think he always has the air of thinking and of acting only with calculation. He is a sly one.” Julien remained deeply humiliated by the misfortune of not having known how to reply to Mme de Rênal. A man like me owes it to himself to make good this failure, and, seizing the moment when they were passing from one room to another, he believed it his duty to give Mme de Rênal a kiss.
