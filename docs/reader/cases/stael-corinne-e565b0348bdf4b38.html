How the scores were assigned

Corinne’s embedded “je vous aime” straightforwardly avows her present love for Oswald and supplies the reason for her devotion and visit. It does not itself undertake a commitment: indeed, her speech resists Oswald’s attempt to construe her conduct as creating a marital obligation. Although the surrounding passage describes an irresistible impulse and intense concern, the words occur in a composed explanatory argument rather than as an affect-driven verbal discharge.

English translation

AI-generated analytical aid, supplied to the annotator; scope: wide context.

Of all that he had said, a single word had struck Oswald to the heart: it was the memory of his mother and of the deep attachment his father had had for her. He had lost her when he was only fourteen years old, but he recalled with profound respect both her virtues and the timid and reserved character of those virtues. “Madman that I am!” he exclaimed when he was alone. “I want to know what sort of wife my father intended for me: and do I not know, since I can summon up the image of my mother, whom he loved so much? What more, then, do I want? And why deceive myself by pretending not to know what he would think now if I could still consult him?” Yet it was dreadful for Oswald to return to Corinne’s home, after what had happened the day before, without saying anything to her that would confirm the feelings he had shown her. His agitation and his distress became so intense that they brought back an attack from which he believed himself cured: the healed blood vessel in his chest reopened. While his frightened servants called for help from every quarter, he secretly wished that the end of his life might put an end to his sorrows. “If only I could die,” he said to himself, “after seeing Corinne again, after she had called me her Romeo!” And tears escaped from his eyes: they were the first that any grief other than his father’s death had wrung from him. He wrote to Corinne of the attack that was keeping him at home, and a few melancholy words concluded his letter. Corinne had begun that very day with very deceptive presentiments: she delighted in the impression she had made on Oswald; and, believing herself loved, she was happy, for apart from that she did not know very clearly what she desired. A thousand circumstances caused the idea of marrying Lord Nelvil to be mingled for her with much fear; and as she was a person more passionate than provident, ruled by the present but little concerned with the future, that day which was to cost her so much suffering had dawned for her as the purest and most serene day of her life. On receiving Oswald’s note, a cruel agitation took possession of her soul: she believed him to be in great danger and set out at once on foot, crossing the Corso at the hour when the entire city promenades there, and entering Oswald’s house in the sight of almost all Roman society. She had not given herself time to reflect; and she had hastened there so quickly that, upon arriving in Oswald’s room, she could no longer breathe or utter a single word. Lord Nelvil understood all that she had just risked in order to see him; and, exaggerating to himself the consequences of this action, which in England would have entirely ruined a woman’s reputation, and all the more so that of an unmarried woman, he felt himself overcome by generosity, love, and gratitude; and, rising, weak though he was, he clasped Corinne to his heart and exclaimed: “Dear friend, no, I will not abandon you, when your feeling for me compromises you! when I must make amends...” Corinne understood his thought; and, interrupting him at once as she gently freed herself from his arms, she said to him, after inquiring about his condition, which had improved: “You are mistaken, my lord; in coming to see you, I am doing nothing that most women in Rome would not have done in my place. I learned that you were ill; you are a stranger here, you know no one here but me; it is for me to care for you. Established conventions are highly respectable when one need sacrifice only oneself to them; but must they not yield to the true and profound feelings aroused by a friend’s danger or suffering? What, then, would a woman’s fate be if those same social conventions, while permitting her to love, forbade only the irresistible impulse that sends one flying to the aid of the person one loves? But, I repeat, my lord, do not fear that by coming here I have compromised myself. By virtue of my age and my talents, I have in Rome the freedom of a married woman. I do not conceal from my friends that I have come to your home; I do not know whether they blame me for loving you, but surely they will not blame me for being devoted to you, when I love you.” On hearing these words, so natural and so sincere, Oswald experienced a confused mixture of different impressions; he was touched by the delicacy of Corinne’s reply, but he was almost vexed that what he had first thought was not true; he would have wished her to have committed for his sake a great fault in the eyes of society, so that this very fault, making it his duty to marry her, might put an end to his uncertainties. He thought resentfully of that freedom of Italian manners, which prolonged his anxiety by leaving him much happiness without imposing any bond upon him. He would have liked honor to command him to do what he desired. These painful thoughts once again caused him dangerous attacks. Corinne, in the most dreadful anxiety, knew how to lavish upon him care full of gentleness and charm. Toward evening, Oswald seemed to have greater difficulty breathing; and Corinne, kneeling beside his bed, supported his head in her arms, although she herself was far more deeply moved than he. He often looked at her with an impression of happiness through his suffering. “Corinne,” he said to her in a low voice, “read to me from this collection, in which my father’s thoughts are written, his reflections on death. Do not think,” he said, seeing Corinne’s terror, “that I believe myself threatened by it; but I am never ill without rereading his consolations, which I seem still to hear from his own lips; and besides, dear friend, in this way I want to let you know what sort of man my father was; you will better understand both my grief and his hold over me, and all that I want to confide to you one day.” Corinne took the collection, from which Oswald never parted, and in a trembling voice she read several pages from it: “You who are righteous, beloved of the Lord, you will speak of death without fear, for it will be for you only a change of dwelling; and the one you leave is perhaps the least of all. O innumerable worlds, which to our eyes fill the infinity of space! unknown communities of God’s creatures, communities of his children, scattered across the firmament and arrayed beneath its vaults! May our praises join with yours: we know nothing of your condition; we know nothing of your first, your second, your last share in the bounties of the Supreme Being; but in speaking of death and life, of time past and time to come, we reach, we touch upon the interests of all intelligent and sentient beings, regardless of the places and distances that separate them. Families of peoples, families of nations, assemblages of worlds, you say with us: Glory to the master of the heavens, to the king of nature, to the God of the universe! Glory and homage to him who can, at his will, transform barrenness into abundance, shadow into reality, and death itself into eternal life! “Ah! Without doubt, the end of the righteous man is the death to be desired; but few among us, few among our forebears, have witnessed it. Where is that man who would present himself without fear before the gaze of the Eternal? Where is that man who has loved God without distraction, who has served him from his youth, and who, upon reaching an advanced age, finds in his memories no cause for anxiety? Where is that man who is moral in all his actions, without ever thinking of praise or of the rewards of public opinion? Where is that man so rare among men, that being so worthy of serving as a model for us all? Where is he? Where is he? Ah! If he exists among us, let our reverence surround him; and ask—you will do well—ask to be present at his death as at the most beautiful of spectacles: only arm yourself with courage, so that you may follow him attentively upon the bed of terror from which he will never rise. He foresees it, he is certain of it, and serenity reigns in his gaze, and his brow seems surrounded by a heavenly aureole: he says with the Apostle: ‘I know in whom I have believed’; and this confidence, as his strength dies away, still animates his features. He already beholds his new homeland; but, without forgetting the one he is about to leave, he belongs to his Creator and his God, without casting far from him the feelings that have charmed his life.”
