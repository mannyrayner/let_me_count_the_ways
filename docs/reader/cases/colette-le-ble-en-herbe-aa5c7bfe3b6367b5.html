“A few more days, three weeks,” Phil said to himself. He wiped the sand from his hands on a clump of wet wild thyme, laden with flowers and little hornets caught by the rain, which waited, numbed, for the next ray of sunlight. He breathed in from his palms the fresh, chaste fragrance, and resisted a wave of weakness, of tenderness, a ten-year-old child’s sadness. But through the windowpane, between the long tears of rain and the whirling corollas of the battered morning glories, he looked at Vinca’s face, that woman’s face which she showed only to him, and which she hid from everyone else behind her fifteen years as a sensible, cheerful young girl. A break in the clouds held the downpour within the cloud, half-opened above the horizon a luminous wound, from which an inverted fan of rays blossomed, a sorrowful white. Philippe’s soul leapt forward to meet this respite, seeking the relief, the release that his tormented sixteen years naïvely demanded. But, facing the sea, he could feel behind him the closed window and Vinca leaning against the pane. “A few more days,” he repeated to himself. “And we shall be separated. What are we to do?” It did not even occur to him that the end of the holidays the year before had made him an unhappy boy, then one calmed by the return to Paris and day school, and resigned to Sunday consolations. The year before, Philippe had been fifteen; each birthday relegates to a murky, wretched past everything that is not Vinca and himself. Did he love her, then, to such an extent? He questioned himself, found no word other than the word love, and angrily flung his hair back from his forehead. “Perhaps it isn’t that I love her all that much, but she is mine! That’s all!” He turned toward the house and shouted into the wind: “Vinca! Come! It isn’t raining anymore!” She opened the door and stood on the threshold like an invalid, raising one shoulder toward her ear with a fearful air. “Come on, come! The sea is ebbing; it will carry the rain away with it!” She bound her hair with a white scarf tied at the nape of her neck and looked like a wounded woman. “Come as far as the Nose, at least; it’s dry under the rock.” She followed him without a word along the customs officers’ path, which ran like a ledge along the cliffside. They trod on peppery oregano and the last fragrances of sweet clover. Below them, the sea snapped like torn flags and unctuously licked the rocks. Its force drove warm gusts up toward the top of the cliff, bearing the smell of mussels and the earthy aroma of the little crevices where wind and bird sow seeds as they fly. They reached their retreat, dry and well sheltered beneath a prow of rock, a rimless platform from which they seemed to be sailing toward the open sea. Philippe sat down beside Vinca, who rested her head on his shoulder. She seemed exhausted and immediately closed her eyes. Her brown, rosy, rounded cheeks, dusted with russet freckles, velvety with close down of a plantlike softness, had grown pale since morning, as had her fresh mouth, always a little cracked like a fruit bitten by the day’s heat. After lunch, instead of meeting the complaints of her “childhood sweetheart” with her usual common sense as an intelligent, stubborn, gentle little bourgeois, she had burst into tears, into desperate confessions, into bitter declarations that hated their youth, the future beyond reach, flight made impossible, resignation unacceptable... She had cried, “I love you!” as one cries “Farewell!” and, “I can no longer leave you!” with eyes full of horror. Love, which had grown before they did, had enchanted their childhood and guarded their adolescence against dubious friendships. Less ignorant than Daphnis, Philippe revered and bullied Vinca like a brother, but cherished her as though, in the Eastern manner, they had been married from the cradle... Vinca sighed and opened her eyes again without lifting her head: “I’m not tiring you, am I, Phil?” He shook his head, admiring, so close to his own, those blue eyes whose blue, each time gentler to his heart, quivered between lashes tipped with gold. “You see,” he said, “the storm is moving down. The sea will still be rough at four in the morning... But we have the clearing now, and this evening a beautiful full-moonrise...” Instinctively, he spoke of improving weather, of calm, leading Vinca toward serene images. But she did not answer. “Will you come and play tennis at the Jallons’ tomorrow?” She shook her head no, her eyes closed again, with sudden fury, as though she were refusing forever to drink, to eat, to live... “Vinca!” Philippe pleaded sternly. “You must. We’ll go.” She half-opened her mouth and swept the sea with the gaze of a condemned woman: “Then we’ll go,” she repeated. “What is the use of not going? What is the use of going? Nothing will change anything.” They both thought of the Jallons’ garden, of tennis, of afternoon tea. Pure and frenzied lovers, they thought of the game that would disguise them, for yet another day, as laughing children, and felt utterly exhausted. “A few more days,” Philippe said to himself, “and we shall be separated. We shall no longer wake beneath the same roof, and I shall see Vinca only on Sundays, at her father’s, at mine, or at the cinema. And I am sixteen. Sixteen and five makes twenty-one. Hundreds and hundreds of days... A few months of holidays, it is true, but their ending is atrocious... And yet she is mine... She is mine...” He then realized that Vinca was slipping from his shoulder. With a gentle, imperceptible, deliberate movement, she was sliding, her eyes closed, down the sloping shelf of rock, so narrow that Vinca’s feet were already dangling over empty space... He understood and did not tremble. He weighed the expediency of what his companion was attempting, and tightened his arm around Vinca’s waist so as not to part himself from her. As he pressed it against himself, he felt the very living, resilient reality, the vigorous perfection of that young girl’s body, ready to obey him in life, ready to draw him into death... “To die? What is the use?... Not yet. Must I depart for the other world without having truly possessed all this, which was born for me?” On that sloping rock, he dreamed of possession as a timid adolescent might dream of it, but also as a demanding man might, an heir grimly resolved to enjoy the goods destined for him by time and human laws. For the first time, he alone decided the fate of them as a couple, free either to abandon them to the waves or to cling to the rocky ledge, like the stubborn seed which, nourished by little, flowered there...
