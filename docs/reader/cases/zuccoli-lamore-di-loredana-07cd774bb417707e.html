Then she realized that gently, gently, with consummate skill, he was removing her hat and veil, and, kneeling again, was pressing his lips to her hands. I have left Filippo. I still love him, I love him always, I love no one but him; but his mother is dying, and I must flee from him.... I have come to take refuge with you.... How hot it is in this room; I feel as though I am suffocating!” Without rising to her feet, she unfastened her fur coat and let it fall around her, so that her slender torso seemed to emerge from that soft white nest spotted with black. Looking around, she saw herself in a mirror; she no longer had her hat, no longer her fur coat; she was undressed, as though she had returned to her own home; and that man was at her feet, mute, humble, and already her master.... The sight revealed her to herself. She sprang upright, exclaiming: “No; what am I doing? If you wish to leave here, I shall not detain you.... Grant me the bitter satisfaction of asking your forgiveness. I have paid for my mistake. I truly was guilty, but I appeared guiltier in your eyes because you believed it a caprice, whereas I loved you and love you with deep feeling.... Do not flee, I implore you....” Loredana sat down again, gathering the white fur coat around herself. As soon as Berto saw her thus, calm and astonished, he left the sitting room and returned precipitately, holding a bottle in one hand and a goblet in the other. Neither he nor the young woman noticed the ridiculousness of that dash; Berto pushed toward Loredana a small table supported by four slender griffins and, pouring, set the goblet beside his guest. “Drink,” he said, “I beg you: it will give you strength.” Loredana had seen that the bubbling liquid was champagne; she made a gesture of refusal and replied: “No, it is not possible; it would make me ill.” Candriani was not listening to her; drunk with joy, able to look at her with the intensity of a desire no longer restrained, he looked over all of her, avidly, from her head to her little feet, whose tips peeped out from beneath the farthest hem of her skirt. “I shall make you forget Filippo,” he said suddenly. “You will no longer love him; I am free and alone, I can devote my entire life to you.... Go on loving him today, it does not matter; it is right that you should love him; it will be for me to erase his image from your heart.” And suddenly he burst out: “I am happy; I am happy to see you here beside me; you remembered that I offered you my friendship in an extreme case, and this consoles me for many sorrows.... Ah, I assure you, Loredana, I am no longer the thoughtless man you once knew! I love you tenderly and hope that I may reassure you....” She cut off his words with a gesture. “No,” she replied. “I shall never love you.” She looked around and, seeing the goblet, raised it to her lips, which she felt were parched by an inner fever. “Listen to me,” she continued imperiously. “I am here not because I love you, not because I believe in your friendship, but because I want to and must ruin myself.” “Loredana, Loredana, for pity’s sake!” Berto interrupted. “I must ruin myself. He is about to commit a crime. His sick mother asked him to abandon me, and he refused.... No, I cannot accept this sacrifice; it is a horrible thing; I must leave him, and in such a way that he will no longer seek me, no longer desire me.... I do not want to harm his mother, who has already suffered so much because of me.... If she were to die, ah, if she were to die, what remorse, what shame!... I must prevent him from dishonoring himself!” Berto, standing upright, listened with a sense of wonder to the young woman, who spoke rapidly, shaken by violent agitation; her initial pallor had given way to a feverish flush that crimsoned her cheeks, made her eyes shine intensely, and reddened her lips, giving them the color of vivid blood. She pleased him so much like that, reclining in the armchair and sunk into the white fur coat, that Berto bent down once more to kiss her hands. Loredana pushed him away. “I wanted to kill myself,” she continued, “but it would have been a new scandal; they might have accused Filippo of my death.... Ah, how malicious everyone is!... I have been told to my face that I am your mistress, that my honesty is worth nothing because no one believes me, that a man is a thief when everyone calls him a thief.... How many frightful things I have learned! And then I thought they were right. The only one who did not doubt me was Filippo; he believes in my love and in my honesty, which everyone laughs at; and therefore he too must cease to believe, so that he may be saved.... I thought that, since they call me your mistress, every effort is useless, and I shall never again be able to free myself of this accusation....” “Do not speak like that,” Berto interrupted, addressing her intimately. “It hurts you too much....” She stared at him with wide eyes; that intimate “you” seemed to her more brutal than a kiss that had suddenly closed her mouth; but Berto noticed, and added: “I beg your pardon; I did not mean to offend you. But as she was about to retrieve it, she stopped as though struck by a thought. Where was she going? To her own home? Filippo would take her back. To Filippo’s home? His mother would die of it. And by that hour Filippo must already have read the few lines Loredana had left him: “I shall never forget you; I shall always love you; the farther away I seem to you, the more wholly yours I shall be....” And after this, would she return to him, her head bowed, like a repentant schoolgirl, and always too late to be forgiven?
