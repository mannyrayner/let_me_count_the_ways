The embedded clause “daß ich dich liebe” presents Werther’s love for Lotte as an actual emotional state, here characterized rhetorically as a worldly sin. It does not itself undertake a commitment. Narrative situation

The original annotator’s interpretation of the supplied context.

In a farewell letter written before his suicide, Werther defies the worldly significance of Lotte’s marriage to Albert and acknowledges his love and desire as a “sin” for which he will punish himself. His fervent claims that Lotte is his and that they will be united eternally reveal possessiveness, religiously framed fantasy, and extreme emotional agitation, but these contextual features do not change the highlighted clause’s core force from an avowal of love into an undertaking or reflexive discharge.

AI-generated analytical aid, supplied to the annotator; scope: wide context.

“For the last time, then, for the last time I open these eyes. They shall, alas, see the sun no more; a gloomy, misty day keeps it veiled from them. Mourn, then, Nature! Your son, your friend, your beloved draws near his end. Lotte, it is a feeling beyond compare, and yet it comes closest to a dawning dream, to say to oneself: this is the last morning. The last! Lotte, I have no sense of the word: the last! Do I not stand here in all my strength, and tomorrow I shall lie stretched out and limp upon the ground. To die! What does that mean? See, we dream when we speak of death. I have seen many a person die; but humanity is so limited that it has no sense of the beginning and end of its existence. Now still mine, yours! Yours, O beloved! And in an instant—separated, parted—perhaps forever?—No, Lotte, no—how can I cease to be? How can you cease to be? We are!—cease to be!—What does that mean? It is yet another word, an empty sound, without feeling for my heart.—Dead, Lotte! Buried in the cold earth, so confined! So dark!—I had a female friend who was my all in my helpless youth; she died, and I followed her corpse and stood at the grave as they lowered the coffin and the ropes, whirring, slipped out from beneath it and darted back up, then the first shovelful rattled down, and the fearful chest gave back a dull sound, and duller and ever duller, and at last was covered!—I fell down beside the grave—seized, shaken, terrified, my innermost being torn apart, but I did not know what was happening to me—what will happen to me—To die! Grave! I do not understand the words! O forgive me! Forgive me! Yesterday! It should have been the last moment of my life. O you angel! For the first time, for the first time wholly without doubt, the blissful feeling glowed through my inmost being: she loves me! She loves me! The holy fire that streamed from your lips still burns upon mine; new, warm bliss is in my heart. Forgive me! Forgive me! Ah, I knew that you loved me, knew it from the first soulful glances, from the first pressure of the hand, and yet, whenever I was away again, whenever I saw Albert at your side, I despaired again amid feverish doubts. Do you remember the flowers you sent me when, in that fatal company, you could not say a word to me or offer me your hand? O, I knelt before them half the night, and they sealed your love to me. But alas! These impressions passed away, just as the feeling of his God’s grace gradually withdraws again from the believer’s soul, though it was bestowed upon him in sacred, visible signs with all the fullness of heaven. All that is transitory, but no eternity shall extinguish the glowing life that I savored yesterday upon your lips, that I feel within me! She loves me! This arm embraced her, these lips trembled upon her lips, this mouth stammered against hers. She is mine! You are mine! Yes, Lotte, forever. And what does it matter that Albert is your husband? Husband! That would be for this world, then—and for this world it would be a sin that I love you, that I would like to tear you from his arms into mine? Sin? Good, and I punish myself for it; I have tasted that sin in all its heavenly bliss, have drawn the balm and strength of life into my heart. From this moment you are mine! Mine, O Lotte! I go before you! I go to my Father, to your Father. To Him I shall lament it, and He will comfort me until you come, and I shall fly to meet you and clasp you and remain with you before the countenance of the Infinite in eternal embraces. I am not dreaming, I am not deluding myself! Near the grave, things grow clearer to me. We shall be! We shall see each other again! See your mother! I shall see her, shall find her, ah, and pour out my whole heart before her! Your mother, your very image.” Toward eleven Werther asked his servant whether Albert had returned. The servant said: yes, he had seen Albert’s horse being led there. Thereupon his master gives him an open note containing the following: “Would you be so kind as to lend me your pistols for a journey I intend to make? Fare you very well!” The dear woman had slept little the previous night; what she had feared was decided, decided in a manner she could neither have foreseen nor feared. Her blood, otherwise so pure and flowing so easily, was in a feverish tumult; a thousand different sensations distracted her beautiful heart. Was it the fire of Werther’s embraces that she felt in her breast? Was it resentment at his audacity? Was it a vexed comparison of her present state with those days of wholly ingenuous, free innocence and carefree confidence in herself? How was she to face her husband, how confess to him a scene that she might so properly acknowledge, and that she nevertheless did not dare acknowledge to herself? They had remained silent toward each other for so long, and was she to be the first to break the silence and, at precisely the wrong time, make so unexpected a disclosure to her husband? She already feared that the mere news of Werther’s visit would make an unpleasant impression upon him, and now this unexpected catastrophe besides! Could she hope that her husband would see it entirely in the proper light, would receive it wholly without prejudice? And could she wish him to read her soul? And yet again, could she dissemble before the man to whom she had always stood open and free like a crystal-clear glass, and from whom she had never concealed, nor had ever been able to conceal, any of her feelings? The one and the other caused her anxiety and placed her in a quandary; and again and again her thoughts returned to Werther, who was lost to her, whom she could not let go, whom—alas!—she had to leave to himself, and who, once he had lost her, had nothing left. How heavily now the estrangement that had taken root between them weighed upon her, though in that moment she could not clearly account for it to herself! Such sensible, such good people began, because of certain secret differences, to remain silent with each other; each reflected upon his own right and the other’s wrong, and their relations became so entangled and embittered that it became impossible to untie the knot at the very critical moment upon which everything depended. Had a fortunate intimacy brought them closer to each other again sooner, had love and forbearance come alive between them in mutual exchange and opened their hearts, perhaps our friend might still have been saved.”
