Narrative situation

The original annotator’s interpretation of the supplied context.

Oswald makes the avowal during an emotionally charged dispute about Corinne’s concealed identity. It functions argumentatively: since she can see that he loves her and is prepared to offer his hand and life, he treats her refusal to disclose herself as cruel. His prior private resolve to marry and protect her strengthens the sincerity and commitment context, while his later distress reveals conflict between this love and paternal or national obligations. None of the suddenness, repetition, or bodily intensity shows that the love words themselves are a reflex-like discharge rather than an impassioned avowal.

“I am delighted to see you,” he said to Lord Nelvil; “I am going to Naples in a fortnight; shall I find you there? I should like to, for I have little time left to stay in Italy, because my regiment must soon embark.—Your regiment?” repeated Lord Nelvil; and he blushed, as though he had forgotten that he had a year’s leave, his regiment not being due to be deployed before then; but he blushed at the thought that Corinne might perhaps make him forget even his duty. “Your own regiment,” continued Mr. Edgermond, “will not be called into active service anytime soon; so recover your health here without anxiety. Before leaving, I saw my young cousin, in whom you take an interest; she is more charming than ever; and in a year, when you return, I do not doubt that she will be the most beautiful woman in England.” Lord Nelvil said nothing, and Mr. Edgermond likewise remained silent on his side. They exchanged a few more words in a rather laconic, though kindly, manner; and Mr. Edgermond was about to leave when he retraced his steps and said: “By the way, my lord, you can do me a favor: I have been told that you know the famous Corinne; and although in general I do not like making new acquaintances, I am altogether curious about that one.—I shall ask Corinne’s permission to take you to her house, since you wish it,” Oswald replied. “Please arrange,” Mr. Edgermond resumed, “for me to see her on a day when she will improvise, sing, or dance in our presence.—Corinne,” said Lord Nelvil, “does not display her talents to strangers in that fashion; she is a woman who is your equal and mine in every respect.—Forgive my mistake,” Mr. Edgermond resumed; “since she is known by no name other than Corinne, and at the age of twenty-six lives entirely alone, without any member of her family, I thought that she lived by her talents and willingly seized the opportunity to make them known.—Her fortune,” Lord Nelvil replied sharply, “is entirely independent, and her soul more so still.” Mr. Edgermond immediately ceased speaking about Corinne, and regretted having mentioned her when he saw that this subject affected Oswald. The English are the men in the world who show the greatest discretion and consideration in everything pertaining to true affections. Mr. Edgermond went away. Lord Nelvil, left alone, could not keep himself from exclaiming in his emotion: “I must marry Corinne; I must be her protector, so that henceforth no one may fail to recognize her for what she is. I shall give her the little that I can give—a rank, a name—while she will shower upon me all the felicities that she alone can bestow on earth.” In this frame of mind he hastened to Corinne’s house, and never had he entered it with a sweeter feeling of hope and love; but, through a natural impulse of timidity, he began the conversation by reassuring himself with insignificant words, among which was the request to bring Mr. Edgermond to her house. At this name, Corinne became visibly troubled, and in an emotional voice refused what Oswald desired. He was singularly astonished by this, and said to her: “I thought that in a house where you receive so many people, the title of my friend would not be grounds for exclusion.—Do not take offense, my lord,” Corinne replied; “believe me, I must have very powerful reasons for not consenting to what you desire.—And will you tell me these reasons?” Oswald resumed. “Impossible!” cried Corinne, “impossible!—So then…” said Oswald; and the violence of his emotion cutting him short, he made to leave. Corinne then, in tears, said to him in English: “In the name of God, if you do not wish to break my heart, do not go.” These words, this accent, deeply stirred Oswald’s soul, and he sat down again at some distance from Corinne, his head resting against an alabaster vase that illuminated her room; then suddenly he said to her: “Cruel woman! You see that I love you, you see that twenty times a day I am ready to offer you both my hand and my life, and you will not tell me who you are! Tell me, Corinne, tell me,” he repeated, holding out his hand to her with the most touching expression of feeling. “Oswald,” cried Corinne, “Oswald, you do not know the harm you are doing me. If I were mad enough to tell you everything, if I were, you would no longer love me.—Great God!” he replied, “what, then, do you have to reveal?—Nothing that makes me unworthy of you; but chance events, but differences between our tastes, our opinions, which once existed, which would no longer exist. Do not require me to make myself known to you; one day perhaps, one day, if you love me enough, if… Ah! I do not know what I am saying,” continued Corinne; “you will know everything, but do not abandon me before hearing me out. Promise me, in the name of your father who dwells in heaven.—Do not utter that name!” cried Lord Nelvil; “do you know whether he unites us or separates us? Corinne sat down beside him; and, holding his hands in hers, gently brought him back to himself. “My dear Oswald,” she said to him, “ask Mr. Edgermond whether he has ever been in Northumberland, or at least whether it is only within the last five years that he has been there; only in that case may you bring him here.” Oswald stared fixedly at Corinne at these words; she lowered her eyes and said nothing. These various questions agitated Oswald’s heart extremely; he was convinced that nothing bad could be discovered in Corinne’s life, but he feared a combination of circumstances that might make her appear guilty in the eyes of others; and what he dreaded most for her was England’s disapproval. He felt strong against that of any other country; but the memory of his father was so intimately joined in his thoughts with his native land that these two feelings heightened one another. Oswald learned from Mr. Edgermond that he had been in Northumberland for the first time the preceding year, and promised to take him to Corinne’s house that very evening. He arrived first to warn her of the notions Mr. Edgermond had formed about her, and asked her to make him feel, through cold and reserved manners, how greatly he had been mistaken.
