Je pensais que j'étais plus que vous inconsolable, et que vous m'oublieriez, quand je vous regretterais toujours. Enfin les circonstances m'enlacèrent; et je ne veux point nier que Lucile ne soit digne et des sentiments qu'elle m'inspire, et de bien mieux encore. Sans doute vous avez à m'opposer une vie de regrets et de douleurs, une fidélité profonde pour un ingrat qui ne la méritait pas; mais songez que la destinée des hommes se complique de mille rapports divers qui troublent la constance du coeur. English translation

AI-generated analytical aid, supplied to the annotator; scope: wide context.

“When you gave me back my freedom, I was angry with you; I returned to the opinions that ordinary men are bound to form upon seeing you. I told myself that so superior a person would easily do without me. Corinne, I have torn your heart apart, I know it; but I believed I was sacrificing no one but myself. I thought that I was more inconsolable than you, and that you would forget me, while I would always mourn your loss. At last circumstances ensnared me; and I do not wish to deny that Lucile is worthy both of the feelings she inspires in me and of far better still. But as soon as I learned of your journey to England and of the misfortune I had caused you, there was nothing left in my life but continual anguish. For four years I sought death amid the war, certain that, upon learning that I was no more, you would find me justified. No doubt you have to set against me a life of regrets and sorrows, a profound fidelity to an ungrateful man who did not deserve it; but consider that men’s destinies are entangled in a thousand different ties that disturb the constancy of the heart. Yet if it is true that I have been able neither to find nor to give happiness; if it is true that I have lived alone since I left you, that I never speak from the depths of my heart, that the mother of my child, she whom for so many reasons I ought to love, remains a stranger to my secrets as to my thoughts; if it is true that a habitual state of sadness has plunged me back into that illness from which your care, Corinne, once rescued me; if I have come to Italy, not to be cured—you do not believe that I love life—but to bid you farewell, will you refuse to see me once, only once? I wish it because I believe I would do you good. It is not my own suffering that determines me. What does it matter if I am utterly miserable! What does it matter if a dreadful weight lies forever upon my heart, if I leave here without having spoken to you, without having obtained your forgiveness! I must be unhappy, and I certainly shall be. But it seems to me that your heart would be relieved if you could think of me as your friend, if you had seen how dear you are to me, if you had felt it in those looks, in that tone of Oswald’s, of that guilty man whose fate has changed more than his heart. “I respect my bonds, I love your sister; but the human heart, strange and inconsistent as it is, can contain both that tenderness and the tenderness I feel for you. I have nothing to tell you about myself that can be written; everything that must be explained condemns me. Nevertheless, if you saw me prostrate myself before you, you would perceive, through all my wrongs and all my duties, what you still are to me, and this conversation would leave you with a gentle feeling. Alas! the health of both of us is very frail, and I do not believe that heaven destines us for a long life. May whichever of us precedes the other feel mourned, feel loved by the friend left behind in this world! The innocent alone should have this joy; but let it be granted to the guilty as well! “Corinne, sublime friend, you who read hearts, divine what I cannot say; understand me as you used to understand me. Let me see you; allow my pale lips to press your weakened hands: ah! I alone did not cause this harm; it is the same feeling that has consumed us both: it is destiny that has struck two beings who loved each other; but it has condemned one of them to guilt, and that one, Corinne, is perhaps not the less to be pitied!” CORINNE’S REPLY. “If all that were required for me to see you was to forgive you, I would not have refused for an instant. I do not know why I feel no resentment against you, although the pain you have caused me makes me shudder with terror. I must still love you, to feel no stirring of hatred; religion alone would not suffice to disarm me in this way. I have had moments when my reason was impaired; others—and they were the sweetest—when I thought I would die before the end of the day from the constriction of heart that oppressed me; and still others when I doubted everything, even virtue: you were its image for me here below, and I no longer had any guide for my thoughts or for my feelings when the same blow struck both admiration and love within me. “What would have become of me without heavenly aid? There was nothing in this world that was not poisoned by the memory of you. One refuge alone remained to me in the depths of my soul. God received me there. My physical strength is declining; but it is not so with the ardor that sustains me. To make oneself worthy of immortality is, I delight in believing, the sole purpose of existence. Happiness, suffering, everything is a means to that end; and you were chosen to uproot my life from the earth: I was bound to it by too strong a tie. “When I learned of your arrival in Italy, when I saw your handwriting again, when I knew that you were there, on the other side of the river, I felt a terrifying tumult in my soul. I had to remind myself constantly that my sister was your wife in order to combat what I felt. I do not conceal it from you: seeing you again seemed to me a happiness, an indefinable emotion, which my newly intoxicated heart preferred to centuries of calm; but Providence did not abandon me in this peril. Are you not another woman’s husband? What, then, could I have to say to you? Was I even permitted to die in your arms? And what would have remained for my conscience if I made no sacrifice, if I still desired one final day, one final hour! Now I shall perhaps appear before God with greater confidence, since I was able to renounce seeing you. This great resolution will bring peace to my soul. Happiness, such as I felt it when you loved me, is not in harmony with our nature: it agitates, it troubles, it is so ready to pass away! But habitual prayer, religious contemplation whose aim is to perfect oneself, to be guided in all things by the sense of duty, is a gentle state, and I cannot know what havoc the mere sound of your voice might wreak in this life of repose that I believe I have attained. You have caused me much pain by telling me that your health was impaired. Ah! I am not the one who tends it, but I am still the one who suffers with you. May God bless your days, my lord; be happy, but be so through piety. A secret communion with the Divinity seems to place within ourselves the being who confides and the voice that answers him; it makes two friends of a single soul. Would you still seek what is called happiness? Ah! will you find anything better than my tenderness? Do you know that in the deserts of the New World I would have blessed my fate if you had allowed me to follow you there? Do you know that I would have served you like a slave? Do you know that I would have prostrated myself before you as before a messenger from heaven, if you had loved me faithfully? Well then, what have you made of so much love? What have you made of this affection unique in the world? A misfortune as unique as itself. Aspire no longer, then, to happiness; do not offend me by believing that you may still attain it. Pray as I do, pray, and may our thoughts meet in heaven.”
