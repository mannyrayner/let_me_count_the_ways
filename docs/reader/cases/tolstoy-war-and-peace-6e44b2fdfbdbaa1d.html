But after that night in Mytishchi, when, in a half-delirium, the woman he longed for appeared before him and when, pressing her hand to his lips, he wept quiet, joyful tears, love for one woman imperceptibly stole into his heart and bound him to life again. Both joyful and anxious thoughts began to come to him. Recalling that moment at the dressing station when he had seen Kuragin, he could no longer return to that feeling: he was now tormented by the question of whether he was alive. And he did not dare ask. His illness followed its physical course, but what Natasha called “this happened to him” occurred two days before Princess Marya’s arrival. It was that last moral struggle between life and death in which death was victorious. It was the unexpected realization that he still cherished life, which appeared to him in his love for Natasha, and a final, vanquished fit of terror before the unknown. It was evening. As usual after dinner, he was in a mildly feverish state, and his thoughts were extraordinarily clear. Sonya was sitting at the table. He dozed off. Suddenly a feeling of happiness enveloped him. “Ah, she has come in!” he thought. Indeed, in Sonya’s place sat Natasha, who had just entered with inaudible steps. Ever since she had begun tending him, he had always experienced this physical sensation of her nearness. She sat in an armchair, turned sideways to him, shielding the candlelight from him with her body, and knitted a stocking. (She had learned to knit stockings after Prince Andrei once told her that no one knew how to tend the sick as well as old nurses who knitted stockings, and that there was something soothing in knitting a stocking.) Her slender fingers rapidly worked the needles, which occasionally clicked against each other, and the pensive profile of her lowered face was clearly visible to him. She made a movement—the ball rolled off her knees. She started, glanced at him, and, shielding the candle with her hand, bent with a careful, supple, precise movement, picked up the ball, and sat down in her former position. He looked at her without moving and saw that after her movement she needed to draw a full breath, but she did not dare do so and breathed carefully. At the Trinity Lavra they had spoken of the past, and he had told her that if he lived, he would thank God forever for his wound, which had brought him together with her again; but since then they had never spoken of the future. “Could it or could it not have been?” he now thought, looking at her and listening to the light metallic sound of the needles. “Can it really be that fate brought me together with her so strangely only for me to die? Can it really be that the truth of life was revealed to me only so that I might live a lie? I love her more than anything in the world. But what am I to do if I love her?” he said, and suddenly, involuntarily, he groaned, from a habit he had acquired during his sufferings. Hearing the sound, Natasha put down the stocking, leaned closer to him, and suddenly, noticing his shining eyes, went up to him with a light step and bent over him. “Are you not asleep?” “No, I have been looking at you for a long time; I felt it when you came in. No one gives me, as you do, that gentle stillness... that light. I feel so much like weeping for joy.” Natasha moved closer to him. Her face shone with rapturous joy. “Natasha, I love you too much. More than anything in the world.” “And I?” She turned away for a moment. “Why too much?” she said. “Why too much?... Well, what do you think, what do you feel in your soul, in your whole soul—will I live? What does it seem to you?” “I am sure, I am sure!” Natasha almost cried out, taking both his hands with a passionate movement. He was silent for a while. “How good that would be!” And, taking her hand, he kissed it. Natasha was happy and agitated; and at once she remembered that this was not allowed, that he needed quiet. “But you have not slept,” she said, suppressing her joy. “Try to fall asleep... please.” He released her hand after pressing it, and she moved over to the candle and again sat down in her former position. Twice she looked back at him; his eyes shone in response to hers. She assigned herself a certain amount of the stocking and told herself that she would not look back until she had finished it. Indeed, soon afterward he closed his eyes and fell asleep. He did not sleep long and suddenly awoke anxiously, in a cold sweat. As he fell asleep, he kept thinking about the same thing he had been thinking about all that time—about life and death. And more about death. He felt himself closer to it. “Love? What is love?” he thought. “Love stands in the way of death. Love is life. Everything, everything I understand, I understand only because I love. Everything is, everything exists, only because I love. Everything is bound together by love alone. Love is God, and to die means for me, a particle of love, to return to the universal and eternal source.” These thoughts seemed comforting to him. But they were only thoughts. Something was lacking in them; there was something one-sidedly personal and intellectual—there was no certainty. And there was the same uneasiness and obscurity. He fell asleep. He dreamed that he was lying in the same room in which he was actually lying, but that he was not wounded and was healthy. Many different faces, insignificant and indifferent, appeared before Prince Andrei. He talked with them, argued about something unnecessary. They were preparing to go somewhere. Prince Andrei vaguely remembered that all this was insignificant and that he had other, more important concerns, but he continued talking, astonishing them with empty, witty words. Little by little, imperceptibly, all these faces began to disappear, and everything was replaced by a single question concerning the closed door. He got up and went to the door to slide the bolt and lock it. Everything depended on whether or not he managed to lock it. And this fear was the fear of death: it was standing behind the door. But at the very moment when he was crawling helplessly and awkwardly toward the door, that dreadful something, already pressing from the other side, was forcing its way through it. Something not human—death—was forcing its way through the door, and he had to hold it back. He seized the door and strained in a final effort—it was no longer possible to lock it—at least to hold it shut; but his strength was weak and clumsy, and, pressed by the dreadful thing, the door opened and closed again. Once more it pressed from the other side. His last, superhuman efforts were in vain, and both halves opened soundlessly. It entered, and it was death. And Prince Andrei died.
