Cependant il était difficile pour moi d'entamer la conversation sur le sujet qui l'amenait. Marguerite le comprit sans doute, car elle reprit: --Je viens vous ennuyer, Armand, parce que j'ai deux choses à vous demander: pardon de ce que j'ai dit hier à Mademoiselle Olympe, et grâce de ce que vous êtes peut-être prêt à me faire encore. Volontairement ou non, depuis votre retour, vous m'avez fait tant de mal, que je serais incapable maintenant de supporter le quart des émotions que j'ai supportées jusqu'à ce matin. Vous aurez pitié de moi, n'est-ce pas? et vous comprendrez qu'il y a pour un homme de cœur de plus nobles choses à faire que de se venger d'une femme malade et triste comme je le suis. Tenez, prenez ma main. J'ai la fièvre, j'ai quitté mon lit pour venir vous demander, non pas votre amitié, mais votre indifférence. En effet, je pris la main de Marguerite. Elle était brûlante, et la pauvre femme frissonnait sous son manteau de velours. Je roulai auprès du feu le fauteuil dans lequel elle était assise. --Croyez-vous donc que je n'ai pas souffert, repris-je, la nuit où, après vous avoir attendue à la campagne, je suis venu vous chercher à Paris, où je n'ai trouvé que cette lettre qui a failli me rendre fou? Comment avez-vous pu me tromper, Marguerite, moi qui vous aimais tant! --Ne parlons pas de cela, Armand, je ne suis pas venue pour en parler. J'ai voulu vous voir autrement qu'en ennemi, voilà tout, et j'ai voulu vous serrer encore une fois la main. Vous avez une maîtresse jeune, jolie, que vous aimez, dit-on: soyez heureux avec elle et oubliez-moi. --Et vous, vous êtes heureuse, sans doute? --Ai-je le visage d'une femme heureuse, Armand? Ne raillez pas ma douleur, vous qui savez mieux que personne quelles en sont la cause et l'étendue. --Il ne dépendait que de vous de n'être jamais malheureuse; si toutefois vous l'êtes comme vous le dites. --Non, mon ami, les circonstances ont été plus fortes que ma volonté. J'ai obéi, non pas à mes instincts de fille, comme vous paraissez le dire, mais à une nécessité sérieuse et à des raisons que vous saurez un jour, et qui vous feront me pardonner. --Pourquoi ne me dites-vous pas ces raisons aujourd'hui? --Parce qu'elles ne rétabliraient pas un rapprochement impossible entre nous, et qu'elles vous éloigneraient peut-être de gens dont vous ne devez pas vous éloigner. --Quelles sont ces gens? --Je ne puis vous le dire. --Alors, vous mentez. Marguerite se leva et se dirigea vers la porte. Je ne pouvais assister à cette muette et expressive douleur sans en être ému, quand je comparais en moi-même cette femme pâle et pleurante à cette fille folle qui s'était moquée de moi à l'Opéra-Comique. --Vous ne vous en irez pas, dis-je en me mettant devant la porte. --Pourquoi? --Parce que, malgré ce que tu m'as fait, je t'aime toujours et que je veux te garder ici. --Pour me chasser demain, n'est-ce pas? Non, c'est impossible! Nos deux destinées sont séparées, n'essayons pas de les réunir; vous me mépriseriez peut-être, tandis que maintenant vous ne pouvez que me haïr. --Non, Marguerite, m'écriai-je en sentant tout mon amour et tous mes désirs se réveiller au contact de cette femme. Non, j'oublierai tout, et nous serons heureux comme nous nous étions promis de l'être. Marguerite secoua la tête en signe de doute, et dit: --Ne suis-je pas votre esclave, votre chien? Faites de moi ce que vous voudrez, prenez-moi, je suis à vous. Une toux sèche et rauque s'ensuivit. --Faites dire à mon cocher, reprit-elle, de reconduire ma voiture. Je descendis moi-même congédier cet homme. Quand je rentrai, Marguerite était étendue devant le feu, et ses dents claquaient de froid. De grosses larmes coulaient de temps en temps de ses yeux et s'arrêtaient sur sa joue, brillantes comme des diamants. Ses bras épuisés s'ouvraient de temps en temps pour me saisir, et retombaient sans force sur le lit. Quand elle fut partie, je fus épouvanté de la solitude dans laquelle elle me laissait. Deux heures après son départ, j'étais encore assis sur le lit qu'elle venait de quitter, regardant l'oreiller qui gardait les plis de sa forme, et me demandant ce que j'allais devenir entre mon amour et ma jalousie. The last time she had come to my rooms, she had sat down in the place where she had just seated herself; only, since that time, she had been another man's mistress; kisses other than mine had touched her lips, toward which, despite myself, my own were reaching, and yet I felt that I loved this woman as much as, and perhaps more than, I had ever loved her. Nevertheless, it was difficult for me to begin the conversation on the subject that had brought her. Marguerite doubtless understood this, for she resumed: “I have come to trouble you, Armand, because I have two things to ask of you: forgiveness for what I said yesterday to Mademoiselle Olympe, and mercy for what you may perhaps be ready to do to me still. Willingly or not, since your return you have caused me so much pain that I would now be incapable of enduring a quarter of the emotions I endured up until this morning. You will take pity on me, will you not? And you will understand that there are nobler things for a man of heart to do than to take revenge on a sick and sorrowful woman such as I am. Here, take my hand. I have a fever; I left my bed to come and ask you, not for your friendship, but for your indifference.” Indeed, I took Marguerite's hand. It was burning hot, and the poor woman was shivering beneath her velvet cloak. I rolled the armchair in which she was sitting nearer the fire. “Do you then believe that I did not suffer,” I replied, “on the night when, after waiting for you in the country, I came to look for you in Paris, where I found nothing but that letter which nearly drove me mad? How could you deceive me, Marguerite—me, who loved you so much?” “Let us not speak of that, Armand; I did not come to speak of it. I wanted to see you as something other than an enemy, that is all, and I wanted to press your hand once more. You have a young, pretty mistress whom you love, they say: be happy with her and forget me.” “And you, you are happy, no doubt?” “Do I have the face of a happy woman, Armand? Do not mock my sorrow, you who know better than anyone its cause and its extent.” “It depended only on you never to be unhappy—if indeed you are, as you say.” “No, my friend, circumstances were stronger than my will. I obeyed, not my instincts as a fallen woman, as you seem to suggest, but a grave necessity and reasons that you will learn one day, and that will make you forgive me.” “Why do you not tell me those reasons today?” “Because they would not bring about an impossible reconciliation between us, and because they might estrange you from people from whom you must not become estranged.” “Who are these people?” “I cannot tell you.” “Then you are lying.” Marguerite rose and made her way toward the door. I could not witness this mute and expressive sorrow without being moved, when I compared within myself this pale, weeping woman with the madcap girl who had mocked me at the Opéra-Comique. “Thou shalt not go,” I said, placing myself in front of the door. “Why?” “Because, despite what thou hast done to me, I still love thee, and I want to keep thee here.” “To turn me out tomorrow, is that it? No, it is impossible! Our two destinies are separate; let us not try to reunite them. You might despise me, whereas now you can only hate me.” “No, Marguerite,” I cried, feeling all my love and all my desires awaken at the touch of this woman. “No, I shall forget everything, and we shall be happy as we had promised ourselves we would be.” Marguerite shook her head as a sign of doubt and said: “Am I not your slave, your dog? Do with me whatever you will; take me, I am yours.” And, removing her cloak and hat, she threw them onto the sofa and began abruptly unfastening the bodice of her dress, for, through one of those reactions so frequent in her illness, the blood was rushing from her heart to her head and choking her. A dry, hoarse cough followed. “Have my coachman told,” she resumed, “to take my carriage back.” I went downstairs myself to dismiss the man. When I returned, Marguerite was lying in front of the fire, and her teeth were chattering with cold. I took her in my arms, undressed her without her making a movement, and carried her, icy cold, to my bed. Then I sat down beside her and tried to warm her beneath my caresses. She did not say a word to me, but she smiled at me. Oh! It was a strange night. Marguerite's whole life seemed to have passed into the kisses with which she covered me, and I loved her so much that, in the midst of the transports of her feverish love, I wondered whether I was not going to kill her so that she might never belong to another. A month of a love like that, in body as in heart, and one would be nothing but a corpse. Daylight found us both awake. Marguerite was livid. She did not say a word. Large tears flowed from her eyes from time to time and stopped upon her cheek, shining like diamonds. Her exhausted arms opened from time to time to grasp me, then fell back strengthless upon the bed. For a moment I believed that I might be able to forget what had happened since my departure from Bougival, and I said to Marguerite: “Wilt thou have us leave, abandon Paris?” “No, no,” she said to me, almost in terror, “we should be too unhappy. I can no longer contribute to thy happiness, but as long as I have a breath left, I shall be the slave of thy whims. At whatever hour of the day or night thou mayst want me, come; I shall be thine. But no longer link thy future with mine; thou wouldst be too unhappy, and thou wouldst make me too unhappy. “For a little while yet I am a pretty girl; take advantage of it, but do not ask anything else of me.” When she had gone, I was terrified by the solitude in which she left me. Two hours after her departure, I was still sitting on the bed she had just left, looking at the pillow that retained the impressions of her form, and wondering what was to become of me between my love and my jealousy. At five o'clock, without knowing what I was going there to do, I made my way to the Rue d'Antin.
