“He had spoken with singular vehemence, and I had remained confounded by what he had told me. The approach of danger, without unsettling him, nevertheless roused him still further, and I could not guess whether it was the truth escaping him, or a lie he was fabricating to avenge himself. Nevertheless, in this uncertainty, I was very careful to spare his life; he was less skilful than I in physical exercises, and ten times I could have plunged my sword into his heart, but I contented myself with wounding him in the arm and disarming him. He appeared touched by my conduct; and, as I led him home, I reminded him of the conversation that had preceded the moment when we fought. He then said to me: ‘I am sorry to have betrayed my cousin’s confidence; peril is like wine, it goes to one’s head; but after all, I console myself for it, because you would not have been happy with Madame d’Arbigny; she is too cunning for you. As for me, that is a matter of indifference; for although I find her charming and her wit pleases me exceedingly, she will never make me do anything to my detriment, and we shall serve each other very well in all things, because marriage will make our interests common. But you, who are romantic, would have been her dupe. You had only to kill me, and I owe you my life; I therefore cannot refuse you the letters I had promised you after my death. Read them, leave for England, and do not be too tormented by Madame d’Arbigny’s sorrows. She will weep, because she loves you; but she will console herself, because she is a woman reasonable enough not to wish to be unhappy, and above all not to wish to appear so. In three months she will be Madame de Maltigues.’ Everything he told me was true: the letters he showed me proved it. I remained convinced that Madame d’Arbigny was not in the condition she had pretended, blushing, to confess to me in order to compel me to marry her, and that in this respect she had shamefully deceived me. She undoubtedly loved me, since she said so in her letters to Monsieur de Maltigues himself; but she flattered him with such art, left him so much hope, and, in order to please him, displayed a character so different from the one she had always shown me, that it was impossible for me to doubt that she was keeping him well disposed toward her, with the intention of marrying him if our marriage did not take place. Such was the woman, Corinne, who has forever cost me the peace of my heart and of my conscience! “I wrote to her as I departed, and I never saw her again; and, as Monsieur de Maltigues had predicted, I have since learned that she married him. But at that time I was far from foreseeing the misfortune that awaited me: I believed I would obtain my father’s forgiveness; I was sure that, when I told him how greatly I had been deceived, he would love me more, since he would know me to be more deserving of pity. After a journey of nearly a month, day and night, across Germany, I arrived in England full of confidence in the inexhaustible goodness of a father. Corinne, as I disembarked, a newspaper announced to me that my father was no more! Twenty months have passed since that moment, and it is still before me like a spectre pursuing me. The letters that formed these words, *Lord Nelvil has just died*, those letters were blazing; the fire of the volcano there before us is less terrifying than they were. Nor is that all; I learned that he had died profoundly distressed by my stay in France, fearing that I might renounce a military career, that I might marry a woman of whom he thought little, and that, by settling in a country at war with my own, I might utterly ruin my reputation in England! Who knows whether these painful thoughts did not shorten his days! Corinne, Corinne, am I not a murderer, am I not? Tell me.—No, she cried, no, you are only unfortunate; it was goodness, it was generosity that carried you away. I respect you as much as I love you: judge yourself within my heart; take it for your conscience. Grief is leading you astray: believe her who cherishes you. Ah! love, such as I feel it, is no illusion: it is because you are the best and most sensitive of men that I admire and adore you.—Corinne, Oswald said to her, I do not deserve this homage; yet it may be that I am not so guilty. My father forgave me before he died; in a final piece of writing from him, addressed to me, I found gentle words. A letter of mine had reached him and had justified me to some degree; but the harm was done, and the grief that came from me had torn his heart. “When I returned to his castle, when his old servants surrounded me, I rejected their consolations, I accused myself before them; I went and prostrated myself upon his grave; there I swore, as though there still remained time for me to make amends, that I would never marry without my father’s consent. Alas! what was I promising to him who was no more! What meaning did those words of my delirium then have! I must at least regard them as an undertaking to do nothing that he would have disapproved of during his life. Corinne, dear friend, why do these words trouble you? My father may have required of me the sacrifice of a dissembling woman, who owed the liking she inspired in me to nothing but her artfulness; but the truest, most natural, and most generous person, she for whom I have felt my first love, the love that purifies the soul instead of leading it astray—why should celestial beings wish to separate me from her? “When I entered my father’s room, I saw his cloak, his armchair, his sword, which were still there, as in former days; still there: but his place was empty, and my cries called to him in vain! This manuscript, this collection of his thoughts, is all that answers me: you already know some passages from it, Oswald said as he gave it to Corinne; I always carry it with me. Read what he wrote about the duty of children toward their parents; read, Corinne: your gentle voice may perhaps make these words familiar to me. Corinne obeyed Oswald’s voice and read what follows: “Ah! how little it takes to make a father and mother advanced in years distrust themselves! They readily believe that they are superfluous upon the earth. Of what use could they believe themselves to you, who no longer ask them for counsel? You live wholly in the present moment; you are confined within it by a ruling passion, and everything that does not relate to that moment appears ancient and obsolete to you. Finally, you are so entirely contained within your own person, in both heart and mind, that, believing yourselves alone to form a historical point, the eternal resemblances between times and men escape your attention; and the authority of experience seems to you a fiction, or a vain guarantee intended solely to sustain the credit of the aged and the final enjoyments of their self-esteem. How mistaken you are! The world, that vast theatre, does not change its actors; it is always man who appears upon its stage; but man does not renew himself, he diversifies himself; and as all his forms depend upon a few principal passions whose circle has long since been traversed, it is rare that, in the small combinations of private life, experience, that science of the past, is not the fruitful source of the most useful lessons.”
