Ambiguity noted by the annotator

“Je vous aime” may here shade toward “I am fond of you” or emphatic personal approval rather than romantic love, and it may also serve a flattering argumentative purpose; these possibilities do not alter its predominantly truth-conditional force.

“As long as my fingers have the strength to hold an iron, you will want for nothing!” cried the poor woman, with the accent of the deepest devotion. “When I was head assistant at Madame Prieur’s, I had as a friend a very well-behaved little girl, Postel’s cousin, Basine Clerget; well, Basine has just told me, while bringing me my fine linen, that she is taking over from Madame Prieur: I shall go and work for her!...” “Ah! you will not work there for long!” Séchard replied. “I have found...” For the first time, the sublime belief in success that sustains inventors and gives them the courage to press forward into the virgin forests of the land of discoveries was greeted by Ève with an almost sorrowful smile, and David bowed his head with a funereal motion. “Oh! my dear, I am not making fun of you, I am not laughing, I do not doubt you!” cried the beautiful Ève, kneeling before her husband. “But I see how right you were to maintain the deepest silence about your experiments, about your hopes. Yes, my dear, inventors must conceal the painful birth of their glory from everyone, even from their wives!... A woman is always a woman. Your Ève could not help smiling when she heard you say, ‘I have found it!...’ for the seventeenth time in a month.” David began to laugh so frankly at himself that Ève took his hand and kissed it reverently. It was a delightful moment, one of those roses of love and tenderness that bloom beside the most arid roads of poverty and sometimes at the bottom of precipices. Ève redoubled her courage when she saw misfortune redouble its fury. Her husband’s greatness, his inventor’s naïveté, the tears she sometimes surprised in the eyes of that man of heart and poetry—all these developed in her an unheard-of power of resistance. Once again she resorted to the means that had already succeeded so well for her. She wrote to Monsieur Métivier, asking him to announce the sale of the printing house, offering to pay him out of the price obtained for it, and begging him not to ruin David with needless costs. Faced with this sublime letter, Métivier played dead: his chief clerk replied that, in Monsieur Métivier’s absence, he could not take it upon himself to halt the proceedings. That was not his employer’s custom in business. Ève proposed renewing the notes while paying all the costs, and the clerk agreed, provided that David Séchard’s father guaranteed them by endorsing them. Ève then went on foot to Marsac, accompanied by her mother and Kolb. She faced the old winegrower; she was charming; she succeeded in smoothing that old face; but when, with trembling heart, she spoke of the endorsement, she saw a complete and sudden change come over that drunkard’s face. “If I allowed my son to put his hand to my lips, to the rim of my cashbox, he would plunge it right down into the depths of my entrails!...” he cried. “Children all eat straight out of the paternal purse. And what did I do, I ask you? I never cost my parents a farthing. Your printing house is empty. The mice and rats are the only ones making impressions there... You are beautiful, you are; I love you; you are a hardworking and careful woman. But my son!... Do you know what David is?... Well, he is an idle scholar. If I had *left* him as I was *left*, without knowing his letters, and had made a *bear* of him, like his father, he would have an income... Oh! that boy is my cross, you see! And unfortunately he is quite unique, for there will never be a second impression of him! In short, he makes you unhappy...” (Ève protested with a gesture of absolute denial.) “Yes,” he continued in answer to the gesture, “you were obliged to hire a wet nurse; grief dried up your milk. I know everything, never fear! You are in court and being drummed about all over town. I was only a *bear*; I am not learned; I was not a foreman with Messieurs Didot, the glory of typography; but I have never received a stamped legal document! Do you know what I say to myself as I go to my vineyards, tending them and harvesting, and attending to my little affairs?... I say to myself: ‘My poor old fellow, you take a great deal of trouble, you pile crown upon crown, you will *leave* fine property behind; it will be for the bailiffs, for the attorneys... or for chimeras... for ideas...’ Look, my child, you are the mother of that little boy who seemed to me to have his grandfather’s snout in the middle of his face when I held him at the baptismal font with Madame Chardon; well, think less about Séchard than about that little rascal... I trust only you... You could prevent the squandering of my property... my poor property...” “But, my dear Papa Séchard, your son will be your glory, and one day you will see him rich through his own efforts and with the Cross of the Legion of Honor in his buttonhole...” “And what’s he going to do to achieve that?” asked the winegrower. “You will see! But in the meantime, would a thousand crowns ruin you?... With a thousand crowns you could put an end to the proceedings... Well, if you have no confidence in him, lend them to me; I shall repay you; you can secure them against my dowry, against my work...” “So David Séchard is being sued?” cried the winegrower, astonished to learn that what he had believed to be slander was true. “That is what comes of knowing how to sign one’s name!... And my rents!... Oh! my little girl, I must go to Angoulême, put my affairs in order, and consult Cachan, my attorney... You did mighty well to come... Forewarned is forearmed!” After a two-hour struggle, Ève was obliged to leave, defeated by this invincible argument: “Women understand nothing about business.” Having come with a vague hope of succeeding, Ève made the journey from Marsac to Angoulême again, almost broken. On returning, she arrived just in time to be served with the judgment ordering Séchard to pay Métivier everything. In the provinces, the presence of a bailiff at the door of a house is an event; but Doublon had been coming much too often of late for the neighborhood not to talk about it. And so Ève no longer dared leave her home; she was afraid of hearing whispers as she passed.
