How the scores were assigned

Lucien’s utterance is primarily a forceful avowal that he loves his friends, offered to rebut their charge that vanity and selfishness infect his friendship. It presents the depth of his affection as true. It contains no distinct undertaking or commitment, and the exclamatory framing supplies intensity but not independent evidence that the love words function as a reflex-like expressive discharge.

Ambiguity noted by the annotator

The narrative casts doubt on the depth, stability, and practical consequences of Lucien’s professed love, but this affects sincerity and characterization rather than the utterance’s truth-conditional mode.

Narrative situation

The original annotator’s interpretation of the supplied context.

The avowal is defensive: Lucien invokes his affection after his friends interpret his eager repayment of their aid as vanity and latent selfishness. They answer that love should be demonstrated through participation in their ethos of generous friendship, implicitly challenging not necessarily the existence of affection but its quality and behavioral force. Lucien subsequently concedes that they are right, while the narrator emphasizes his capacity for contradictory resolutions and facile movement across feelings. This creates possible self-deception or unstable sincerity, without changing the core utterance into anything other than an emphatic avowal.

D'Arthez ne connaît que les libraires de science ou de spécialités, qui n'ont aucune prise sur les éditeurs de nouveautés. Horace, Fulgence Ridal et Bridau travaillent dans un ordre d'idées qui les met à cent lieues des libraires. Pour toi, les petits pois devraient pousser tout accommodés au beurre. —Ils ne poussent ainsi que pour les fils aînés des pairs de France, dit Michel Chrestien. Mais, nous autres, nous les semons, les arrosons et les trouvons meilleurs. La conversation devint plaisante, et changea de sujet. Ces esprits perspicaces, ces cœurs délicats cherchèrent à faire oublier cette petite querelle à Lucien, qui comprit dès lors combien il était difficile de les tromper. Il arriva bientôt à un désespoir intérieur qu'il cacha soigneusement à ses amis, en les croyant des mentors implacables. Son esprit méridional, qui parcourait si facilement le clavier des sentiments, lui faisait prendre les résolutions les plus contraires.

“My friend, your letter made us all weep. Let those noble hearts toward whom your good angel is guiding you know this: a mother and a poor young woman will pray to God for them morning and evening; and if the most fervent prayers rise to His throne, they will obtain some favors for all of you. Yes, my brother, their names are engraved upon my heart. Ah! I shall see them someday. I shall go, even if I have to make the journey on foot, to thank them for their friendship toward you, for it has poured balm, as it were, upon my raw wounds. Here, my friend, we are working like poor laborers. My husband, that great unknown man whom I love more with each passing day as, from moment to moment, I discover new riches in his heart, is neglecting his printing house, and I can guess why: your poverty, ours, and our mother’s are killing him. Our adored David is like Prometheus devoured by a vulture, a sallow grief with a sharp beak. As for him, the noble man, he scarcely thinks of it; he has hopes of a fortune. He spends all his days conducting experiments on the manufacture of paper; he has asked me to attend to the business in his stead, in which he helps me as much as his preoccupation allows. Alas! I am with child. This event, which would have filled me with joy, saddens me in the situation in which we all find ourselves. My poor mother has grown young again; she has recovered the strength needed for her exhausting work as a nurse. Were it not for our financial worries, we should be happy. Old Father Séchard will not give his son a farthing; David went to see him to borrow a little money from him so as to help you, for your letter had driven him to despair. ‘I know Lucien; he will lose his head and do foolish things,’ he said. I scolded him thoroughly. My brother, fail in any way whatsoever?... I replied to him, Lucien knows that I should die of grief. Without David’s suspecting it, my mother and I have pawned a few things; my mother will redeem them as soon as she comes into some money. In this way we have managed to raise one hundred francs, which I am sending you by the coach service. If I did not answer your first letter, do not be angry with me, my friend. We were in a situation that kept us up at night; I was working like a man. Ah! I did not know I had so much strength. Madame de Bargeton is a woman without soul or heart; even if she no longer loved you, she owed it to herself to protect and help you after tearing you from our arms only to cast you into that dreadful Parisian sea, where one needs a blessing from God to encounter true friendships amid those waves of men and interests. She is not to be regretted. I wanted some devoted woman beside you, a second self of mine; but now that I know you have friends who carry on our feelings, I am at peace. Spread your wings, my beloved, beautiful genius! You will be our glory, as you are already our love. “EVE.” “My darling child, after what your sister has told you, I can only bless you and assure you that, alas! my prayers and thoughts are filled with you alone, to the detriment of those whom I see; for there are hearts in which the absent are in the right, and so it is in the heart of “YOUR MOTHER.” Thus, two days later, Lucien was able to repay his friends the loan they had so graciously offered him. Perhaps life had never seemed more beautiful to him, but the stirring of his self-esteem did not escape his friends’ penetrating eyes and delicate sensibility. “You would think you were afraid of owing us anything,” exclaimed Fulgence. “Oh! the pleasure he displays is very serious in my eyes,” said Michel Chrestien; “it confirms the observations I have made: Lucien is vain.” “He is a poet,” said d’Arthez. “Do you hold it against me that I feel something as natural as this?” “We must give him credit for not having concealed it from us,” said Léon Giraud. “He is still candid; but I am afraid that later he may come to fear us.” “And why?” asked Lucien. “We read your heart,” replied Joseph Bridau. “There is in you,” Michel Chrestien told him, “a diabolical mind with which you will justify in your own eyes the things most contrary to our principles: instead of being a sophist of ideas, you will be a sophist of action.” “Ah! You will never be in agreement with yourself.” “And on what, then, do you base your indictment?” asked Lucien. “Your vanity, my dear poet, is so great that you even put it into your friendship!” exclaimed Fulgence. “All vanity of that kind betrays a dreadful selfishness, and selfishness is the poison of friendship.” “Oh! my God,” exclaimed Lucien, “you cannot know how much I love you.” “If you loved us as we love one another, would you have shown such eagerness and made such a show of returning what it gave us so much pleasure to give you?” “Here we do not lend anything to one another; we give,” Joseph Bridau told him bluntly. “Do not think us harsh, my dear child,” Michel Chrestien told him; “we are being farsighted. We are afraid that one day we shall see you preferring the joys of some petty revenge to the joys of our pure friendship. Read Goethe’s Tasso, the greatest work of that splendid genius, and you will see in it that the poet loves brilliant fabrics, feasts, triumphs, splendor: well then, be Tasso without his madness. D’Arthez knows only publishers of scientific or specialized works, who have no influence over publishers of new literature. Horace, Fulgence Ridal, and Bridau work in a realm of ideas that places them a hundred leagues away from publishers. I must decide on a course.” “Then adhere to ours: suffer!” said Bianchon. “Suffer courageously and put your trust in Work!” “But what is only suffering for you is death to me,” Lucien said sharply. “Before the cock has crowed three times,” said Léon Giraud with a smile, “this man will have betrayed the cause of Work for that of Idleness and the vices of Paris.” “Where has work brought you?” said Lucien, laughing. “When one leaves Paris for Italy, one does not find Rome halfway there,” said Joseph Bridau. “For you, green peas ought to grow already dressed with butter.” “They grow that way only for the eldest sons of peers of France,” said Michel Chrestien. “But we sow and water them ourselves, and find them better.” The conversation became jocular and moved to another subject. Those discerning minds, those sensitive hearts sought to make Lucien forget this little quarrel, and from then on he understood how difficult it was to deceive them. He soon fell into an inward despair that he carefully concealed from his friends, believing them to be implacable mentors. His southern temperament, which ran so easily over the keyboard of feelings, led him to make the most contradictory resolutions.
