She was gazing toward where he had gone, to that other side of life. And that side of life, of which she had never thought before, which had previously seemed so distant and incredible to her, was now closer and dearer to her, more comprehensible, than this side of life, in which everything was either emptiness and destruction, or suffering and humiliation. She gazed toward where she knew he was; but she could not see him otherwise than as he had been here. She saw him again just as he had been in Mytishchi, at Troitsa, in Yaroslavl. She saw his face, heard his voice, and repeated his words and her own words spoken to him, and sometimes invented for herself and for him new words that might have been said then. There he is, lying in an armchair in his little velvet coat, resting his head on his thin, pale hand. His chest is terribly sunken and his shoulders raised. His lips are firmly compressed, his eyes gleam, and a wrinkle springs up and vanishes on his pale forehead. One of his legs is trembling rapidly, almost imperceptibly. Natasha knows that he is struggling with agonizing pain. “What is this pain? Why pain? What does he feel? How does it hurt him?” Natasha thinks. He noticed her attention, raised his eyes, and, without smiling, began to speak. “One thing is terrible,” he said. “It is to bind oneself forever to a suffering person. It is eternal torment.” And he looked at her with a searching gaze. Natasha, as always, answered then before she had time to think about what she was answering; she said: “It cannot go on like this, it will not; you will get well—completely.” She now saw him again and now relived everything she had felt then. She remembered his long, sad, stern gaze at those words and understood the meaning of reproach and despair in that prolonged gaze. “I agreed,” Natasha now said to herself, “that it would be terrible if he were always to remain suffering. I said it then only because it would have been terrible for him, but he understood it differently. He thought it would have been terrible for me. Even then he still wanted to live—he was afraid of death. And I said it to him so crudely, so stupidly. I did not mean that. I meant something entirely different. If I had said what I meant, I would have said: even if he were dying, were dying all the time before my eyes, I would be happy compared with what I am now. Now... There is nothing, no one. Did he know that? No. He did not know and never will know. And now it can never, never be put right.” And again he said the same words to her, but now, in her imagination, Natasha answered him differently. She stopped him and said: “Terrible for you, but not for me. You must know that without you there is nothing in life for me, and to suffer with you is the greatest happiness for me.” And he took her hand and pressed it as he had pressed it on that terrible evening, four days before his death. And in her imagination she said to him still other tender, loving words that she could have said then. “I love you... you... I love you, I love you...” she said, convulsively clasping her hands, clenching her teeth with fierce effort. And sweet sorrow enfolded her, and tears were already coming into her eyes, but suddenly she asked herself: to whom was she saying this? Where was he, and who was he now? And again everything was veiled by dry, unyielding bewilderment, and again, tensely drawing her brows together, she peered toward where he was. And now, any moment, it seemed to her, she would penetrate the mystery... But at the very moment when the incomprehensible seemed about to be revealed to her, the loud rattle of the door’s latch struck painfully on her hearing. Quickly and incautiously, with a frightened expression on her face that had nothing to do with Natasha, the maid Dunyasha entered the room. “Please come to Papa, quickly,” Dunyasha said with a peculiar, animated expression. “A misfortune, concerning Pyotr Ilyich... a letter,” she uttered with a sob. All her own people—her father, her mother, Sonya—were so close to her, so familiar, so commonplace, that all their words and feelings seemed to her an insult to the world in which she had lately been living, and she was not only indifferent to them, but looked at them with hostility.
