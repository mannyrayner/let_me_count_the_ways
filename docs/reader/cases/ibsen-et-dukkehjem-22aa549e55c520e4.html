How the scores were assigned

Nora’s “Jeg elsker deg ikke mer” (“I no longer love you”) is a direct, explicit avowal that her loving emotional state has ceased. Its core force is strongly truth-conditional. Although it helps explain and communicate the end of the marriage, the utterance itself does not undertake that separation; Nora expresses that decision separately. Her controlled insistence that the conviction is clear and certain supplies no independent expressive/reflexive force.

Jeg må se å få erfaring, Torvald. HELMER. Forlate ditt hjem, din mann og dine børn! Og du tenker ikke på hva folk vil si. NORA. Det kan jeg ikke ta noe hensyn til. Jeg vet bare det blir nødvendig for meg. HELMER. Å, det er opprørende. Således kan du svikte dine helligste plikter. NORA. Hva regner du da for mine helligste plikter? HELMER. Og det skal jeg behøve å si deg! Er det ikke pliktene imot din mann og dine børn? NORA. Jeg har andre likså hellige plikter. HELMER. Det har du ikke. Hvilke plikter skulle det være. NORA. Pliktene imot meg selv. HELMER. Du er først og fremst hustru og mor. NORA. Det tror jeg ikke lenger på. Jeg tror at jeg er først og fremst et menneske, jeg, likså vel som du, - eller iallfall at jeg skal forsøke på å bli det. Jeg vet nok at de fleste gir deg rett, Torvald, at det står noe slikt i bøkene. Men jeg kan ikke lenger la meg nøye med hva de fleste sier, og hva der står i bøkene. Jeg må selv tenke over de ting og se å få rede på dem. HELMER. Du skulle ikke ha rede på din stilling i ditt eget hjem? Har du ikke i sånne spørsmål en usvikelig veileder? Har du ikke religionen? NORA. Akk, Torvald, jeg vet jo slett ikke riktig hva religionen er. HELMER. Hva er det du sier! NORA. Jeg vet jo ikke annet enn hva presten Hansen sa da jeg gikk til konfirmasjonen. Han fortale at religionen var det og det. Når jeg kommer bort fra alt dette her og blir ensom, så vil jeg undersøke den sak også. Jeg vil se om det var riktig hva presten Hansen sa, eller iallfall om det er riktig for meg. HELMER. Å, slikt er dog uhørt av en så ung kvinne! Men kan ikke religionen rettlede deg, så la meg dog ryste opp i din samvittighet. For moralsk følelse har du dog? Eller, svar meg, - har du kanskje ingen? NORA. Ja, Torvald, det er ikke godt å svare på det. Jeg vet det jo slett ikke. Jeg er ganske i villrede med de ting. Jeg vet bare at jeg har en ganske annen mening om slikt noe enn du. Jeg hører også nu at lovene er annerledes enn jeg tenkte; men at de love skulle være riktige, det kan jeg umulig få i mitt hode. En kvinne skal altså ikke ha rett til å skåne sin gamle døende far, eller til å redde sin manns liv! Slikt tror jeg ikke på. HELMER. Du taler som et barn. Du forstår ikke det samfunn du lever i. NORA. Nei, det gjør jeg ikke. Men nu vil jeg sette meg inn i det. Jeg må se å komme efter hvem der har rett, samfunnet eller jeg. HELMER. Du er syk, Nora; du har feber; jeg tror nesten du er fra sans og samling. NORA. Jeg har aldri følt meg så klar og sikker som i natt. HELMER. Og klar og sikker forlater du din mann og dine børn? NORA. Ja; det gjør jeg. HELMER. Så er kun én forklaring mulig. NORA. Hvilken? HELMER. Du elsker meg ikke mer. NORA. Nei, det er just tingen. HELMER. Nora! - Og det sier du! NORA. Å, det gjør meg så ondt, Torvald; for du har alltid vært så snill imot meg. Men jeg kan ikke gjøre ved det. Jeg elsker deg ikke mer. HELMER (med tilkjempet fatning). Er dette også en klar og sikker overbevisning? NORA. Ja, fullkommen klar og sikker. Det er derfor jeg ikke vil være her lenger. HELMER. Og vil du også kunne gjøre meg rede for hvorved jeg har forspilt din kjærlighet? NORA. Ja, det kan jeg godt. Det var i aften da det vidunderlige ikke kom; for da så jeg at du ikke var den mann jeg hadde tenkt meg. HELMER. Forklar deg nøyere; jeg begriper deg ikke. NORA. Jeg har ventet så tålmodig nu i åtte år; for Herregud, jeg innså jo nok at det vidunderlige kommer ikke sånn til hverdags. Så brøt dette knusende inn over meg; og da var jeg så usvikelig viss på: nu kommer det vidunderlige. Da Krogstads brev lå der ute, - aldri falt det meg med en tanke inn at du kunne ville bøye deg under dette menneskes vilkår. Jeg var så usvikelig viss på at du ville si til ham: gjør saken bekjent for hele verden. Og når det var skjedd - HELMER. Ja hva så? Når jeg hadde prisgitt min egen hustru til skam og skjensel -! NORA. Når det var skjedd, da tenkte jeg så usvikelig sikkert at du ville tre frem og ta alt på deg og si: jeg er den skyldige. HELMER. Nora -! NORA. Du mener at jeg aldri ville tatt imot et slikt offer av deg? Nei, det forstår seg. Men hva ville mine forsikringer gjelde like overfor dine? - Det var det vidunderlige som jeg gikk og håpet på i redsel. Og for å hindre det, var det at jeg ville ende mitt liv. HELMER. Jeg skulle gladelig arbeide netter og dage for deg, Nora, - bære sorg og savn for din skyld. Men der er ingen som ofrer sin ære for den man elsker. NORA. Det har hundre tusen kvinner gjort. HELMER. Å, du både tenker og du taler som et uforstandig barn. NORA. La gå. Men du hverken tenker eller taler som den mann jeg skal kunne slutte meg til. Da din forskrekkelse var over, - ikke for hva der truet meg, men for hva du selv var utsatt for, og da hele faren var forbi, - da var det for deg som om slett ingen ting var skjedd. English translation

AI-generated analytical aid, supplied to the annotator; scope: wide context.

NORA. Tomorrow I am going home—I mean, to my old hometown. It will be easiest for me there to find something or other to do. HELMER. Oh, you blind, inexperienced creature. NORA. I must try to gain experience, Torvald. HELMER. Leave your home, your husband, and your children! And you do not think about what people will say. NORA. I cannot take that into consideration. I know only that it will be necessary for me. HELMER. Oh, it is outrageous. That you can betray your most sacred duties in this way. NORA. What, then, do you consider my most sacred duties? HELMER. And should I need to tell you that! Are they not your duties toward your husband and your children? NORA. I have other duties just as sacred. HELMER. You do not. What duties could those be? NORA. My duties toward myself. HELMER. First and foremost, you are a wife and mother. NORA. I no longer believe that. I believe that first and foremost I am a human being, I, just as much as you are—or at any rate that I must try to become one. I know very well that most people agree with you, Torvald, and that something of the sort is written in the books. But I can no longer be satisfied with what most people say and what is written in the books. I must think these things over for myself and try to understand them. HELMER. Should you not understand your position in your own home? Do you not have an infallible guide in such matters? Do you not have religion? NORA. Oh, Torvald, I really do not properly know what religion is at all. HELMER. What are you saying! NORA. I know nothing beyond what Pastor Hansen said when I went to confirmation classes. He explained that religion was this and that. When I get away from all this here and am alone, I shall examine that matter too. I shall see whether what Pastor Hansen said was right, or at any rate whether it is right for me. HELMER. Oh, such a thing is unheard of from so young a woman! But if religion cannot guide you, then at least let me awaken your conscience. Surely you have some moral sense? Or answer me—perhaps you have none? NORA. Yes, Torvald, that is not easy to answer. I really do not know at all. I am completely at a loss about such things. I know only that I have a very different opinion of such matters from yours. I now hear, too, that the laws are different from what I thought; but that those laws should be right is something I cannot possibly get into my head. So a woman is not to have the right to spare her old, dying father, or to save her husband’s life! I do not believe in such things. HELMER. You speak like a child. You do not understand the society in which you live. NORA. No, I do not. But now I intend to learn about it. I must try to find out who is right, society or I. HELMER. You are ill, Nora; you have a fever; I almost believe you are out of your mind. NORA. I have never felt so clear and certain as I do tonight. HELMER. And clear and certain, you are leaving your husband and your children? NORA. Yes; I am. HELMER. Then only one explanation is possible. NORA. Which one? HELMER. You no longer love me. NORA. No, that is precisely it. HELMER. Nora!—And you say that! NORA. Oh, it hurts me so much, Torvald; for you have always been so kind to me. But I cannot help it. I no longer love you. HELMER (with forced composure). Is this also a clear and certain conviction? NORA. Yes, perfectly clear and certain. That is why I do not wish to remain here any longer. HELMER. And will you also be able to explain to me how I have forfeited your love? NORA. Yes, I can readily do that. It was this evening, when the wonderful thing did not happen; for then I saw that you were not the man I had imagined you to be. HELMER. Explain yourself more fully; I do not understand you. NORA. I have waited so patiently for eight years now; for, dear God, I understood well enough that the wonderful thing does not happen every day. Then this crushing blow came upon me; and then I was infallibly certain: now the wonderful thing will happen. When Krogstad’s letter lay out there, it never once entered my mind that you might be willing to submit to that man’s terms. I was infallibly certain that you would say to him: make the matter known to the whole world. And when that had happened— HELMER. Yes, and then what? When I had exposed my own wife to shame and disgrace—! NORA. When that had happened, I thought with infallible certainty that you would step forward and take everything upon yourself and say: I am the guilty one. HELMER. Nora—! NORA. You think I would never have accepted such a sacrifice from you? No, of course not. But what would my assurances have counted for against yours?—That was the wonderful thing for which I went on hoping in terror. And it was to prevent it that I wanted to end my life. HELMER. I would gladly work nights and days for you, Nora—bear sorrow and privation for your sake. But no one sacrifices his honor for the person he loves. NORA. A hundred thousand women have done so. HELMER. Oh, you both think and speak like a foolish child. NORA. Let that be so. But you neither think nor speak like the man to whom I could bind myself. When your fright was over—not at what threatened me, but at what you yourself were exposed to—and when all the danger was past, then for you it was as though nothing at all had happened. I was exactly as before, your little songbird, your doll, whom from then on you would carry in your hands with twice the care, since it was so fragile and frail. (rises.) Torvald—in that moment it dawned on me that for eight years I had lived here with a strange man, and that I had had three children—. Oh, I cannot bear to think of it! I could tear myself to pieces. HELMER (heavily). I see it; I see it. An abyss has indeed opened between us.—Oh, but Nora, could it not be filled in? NORA. As I am now, I am no wife for you. HELMER. I have the strength to become another man. NORA. Perhaps—if your doll is taken away from you. HELMER. Oh, to part—to part from you! No, no, Nora, I cannot grasp that thought. NORA (goes into the room on the right). Then it is all the more certain that it must happen. (she returns with her outer clothing and a small traveling bag, which she sets on the chair by the table.) HELMER. Nora, Nora, not now! Wait until tomorrow. NORA (puts on her coat). I cannot spend the night in a strange man’s rooms. HELMER. But could we not live here as brother and sister—?
