# On saying “I love you”

*Background essay originally posted on Goodreads circa 2014. Reformatted as
Markdown for this project; extended copyrighted extracts have been summarized
or omitted.*

> *Better with love to seal the love-sure lips*<br>
> *For surely love was, before words were made*<br>
> *And no word given, no word broken*
>
> — Robert Graves

What does someone mean when they say “I love you”? First of all, is there anything interesting to discuss? We can easily imagine objections from several different quarters. A common-sense response might be to dismiss the question altogether. Of course everyone knows what “I love you” means! If you claim you’re not sure, you’re either being willfully obtuse, or you are some kind of emotionally damaged person, and I feel very sorry for you. A different kind of objection might come from someone who was familiar with the literature on the formal semantics of language. Let’s suppose that you’re John (*j*), and you are addressing your remark to Mary (*m*). Then “I love you” is true if and only if the first-order formula

> *love′(j, m)*

is true. End of story.

But is either response convincing? Let’s begin with the common-sense one, and consider a concrete case. Suppose, for example, that Mary is walking along with John, and suddenly asks him “Do you love me?” John responds “I don’t know what ‘love’ means”. If Mary is a typical sophisticated Western woman, there will be various things she might think at this point. Perhaps she will feel that John is playing games with her and be annoyed. Perhaps she will be worried that their relationship is in trouble, or wish that he would be more open about his feelings, or more ready to commit. But her reaction will be different from that which one could reasonably expect if she had asked a question that was structurally identical, but used another verb. For example, if she had asked “Do you admire me?” and John had replied “I don’t know what ‘admire’ means”, then I, at least, would immediately guess that John’s native language wasn’t English, and that Mary had just happened to use a word he didn’t know. In this case, it might well be constructive for Mary to give him a dictionary definition of “admire”. In the case of “love”, Mary would be much less likely to give a dictionary definition; if she did, it would probably be ironic, or a move in an emotional game. It’s implausible that it would be a straightforward attempt to explain to John the meaning of a word he didn’t happen to know.

In fact, it is reasonably common for people to claim that they don’t know what “love” means—common enough, even, for this statement to have become a kind of cliché. When I searched Google just now, I found over 250,000 hits that matched the string “I don’t know what love means”. In contrast, I only found 6 for “I don’t know what hate means” and 2 for “I don’t know what admire means”. There appears to be objective support for the claim that “love” is somehow special here, and that people do have some reason to be confused about what it means, while much less confusion exists concerning other, apparently similar, verbs.

Moving on to the formal semantic argument, it is not in fact obvious that a straightforward truth-conditional account of “I love you” is the appropriate one; it just so happens that “John loves Mary” is one of the standard example sentences in the semantics literature, and that the simple account has been perpetuated as part of an informal tradition. If one stops and thinks about it for a moment, it is *a priori* quite conceivable that the statement might better be read as some kind of performative, on the same level as “I name this ship *The Cutty Sark*”, or “I now pronounce you man and wife”. In general, plenty of statements are not best considered in terms of truth-conditional accounts, and it is rational to consider the evidence for and against truth-conditional accounts in specific instances. Here, I will argue that there is a reasonable prima facie case to be made for all of the following semantic treatments of “I love you”:

**A truth-conditional account.** The speaker is directly reporting on their sensations and/or mental state. An approximate paraphrase might be “I have warm fuzzy feelings about you”. The statement is either true or false. If the speaker is experiencing said feelings, they are telling the truth. If they are not, they are lying.

**A performative account.** The speaker is making an undertaking with regard to the addressee. An approximate paraphrase might be “I promise to value and cherish you for the indefinite future”. The statement is felicitous or infelicitous. If the speaker sincerely intends their promise, and keeps it, the statement is clearly felicitous. If they do not sincerely intend their promise and do not keep it, the statement is clearly infelicitous (they have been duplicitous). If they sincerely intend the promise but do not keep it, or do not sincerely intend the promise, but end up keeping it anyway, the status is unclear.

**An expletive account.** For example, if you hit your thumb with a hammer and say “Shit!” it is hard to claim that either a truth-conditional or a performative account is appropriate. Intuitively, one just says it as a reflex. On this account, “I love you” is simply something that people may find themselves saying in response to certain stimuli. It has no overt, or intended, meaning; it cannot be regarded as true, false, felicitous or infelicitous. If it does have a meaning, that meaning can only be construed externally, in terms of the type of situation in which the response is typically produced. So it still may be possible to say “I was surprised that she said ‘I love you’ then”, but stronger claims are not possible.

In theory, one could investigate the different possible interpretations by performing an experiment on a large body of subjects. You would ask them when they’d said “I love you”, and for what reasons. It’s obvious, though, that an experiment of this kind would be difficult or impossible to organise. The next best thing is to look at works of literature. (I will interpret “literature” in a broad sense, to include e.g. movies.) If the piece in question is at all well-known, people will have opinions about what induced the character in question to say the words.

So let’s look at some examples. One possibility that’s worth considering is that the problem doesn’t exist, because all three accounts in practice coincide. As it happens, this is the premise of the Tom Hanks/Daryl Hannah movie *Splash*.

> **Splash**
>
> Near the beginning, Tom Hanks is having a fraught telephone conversation with his girlfriend Victoria. “What do you mean, do I love you?” he asks. He’s clearly been put on the spot. “Well, do you love me? Huh? Huh? Well, there you go then!” But this doesn’t work out well, and shortly afterwards we find she’s left him.
>
> Later, after Hanks has met Daryl Hannah, she unexpectedly gives him an enormous fountain as a present. “Why did you do that?” he asks, bemused. “Because I love you,” she answers. Hanks is taken aback by this forthright declaration. “I love... this present!” he stammers, as usual avoiding the fatal words. Then he reconsiders. “I love *you*, Madison!” He’s surprised he wants to say it.

The implication, made clear by other supporting evidence in the movie, is that Hanks is simply incapable of saying “I love you” to anyone except Hannah. She is the only woman who inspires the right feelings in him, and he can only say the words when he is having those feelings. This is one of the things that makes *Splash* so charming. Unfortunately, most people would also agree that this doesn’t mirror the world we live in. If we appreciate the movie, we do so because it describes an idealised world we wish existed, but which we know perfectly well doesn’t.

Perhaps, then, we could argue that one of the accounts is the “correct” one, and that the others are somehow mistaken. However, it isn’t hard to find plausible examples to support each alternative. Let’s begin with the truth-conditional account.

> **The Reader**
>
> In the movie version of *The Reader*, Michael and Hannah have just had a huge fight. He has no idea what her feelings for him are.
>
> “Do you love me?” he asks angrily.
>
> She inclines her head unwillingly in a brief nod. As the story unfolds, it becomes clear that she is an unusually truthful person.

Here, I think it’s pretty clear that Hannah is doing no more than telling Michael what she feels. She isn’t making a promise, and she isn’t saying the words as a reflexive reaction. It’s just a statement of fact. Similarly, consider the following Eurythmics track:

> **Ball and Chain**
>
> [Extended song lyrics omitted from this repository copy.]

Here, I would again say the singer is only expressing how she feels. She isn’t promising anything, and in fact she would rather not feel this way. It’s simply a fact that she can do nothing about.

But it’s just as easy to find examples which need to be interpreted in terms of “I love you” being some kind of promise:

> **Ghost**
>
> In *Ghost*, every time Demi Moore tells Patrick Swayze that she loves him, he replies “Ditto”. She clearly finds this very irritating.

Here, the implication is that Moore wants Swayze to say “I love you”, and express his commitment to her; but Swayze is reluctant to do so, even though the movie makes it clear that he has very warm, loving feelings for her.

Similarly:

> **Postcards from the Edge**
>
> In the movie *Postcards from the Edge*, Dennis Quaid says to Meryl Streep “I think I love you”. Streep replies “When will you know for sure?”

Streep’s reply is witty because she, again, isn’t interested in knowing about Quaid’s feelings; she wants a promise of commitment. The concept of “knowing for sure” makes no sense with a performative. You can’t, except as a joke, say “I think I pronounce you man and wife” or “I think I name this ship *The Cutty Sark*”.

And there are also examples where “I love you” is just an exclamation:

> **Sleeping Around**
>
> In the second video that Catherine Townsend posted on [her Sleeping Around blog homepage](http://sleeping-around.blogspot.com/), she shows a typical seduction, contrasting what she says and what she is really thinking throughout the performance. At the end, she says “I think I love you”. She simultaneously thinks “I can’t believe I just said that!”

This time, the joke is that Townsend has no idea why she said it: it just came out in the heat of the moment.

Similarly:

> **Vernon God Little**
>
> In *Vernon God Little*, Vern is on the run and out of money. Suddenly, his dream girl shows up with $600. His ironic interior monologue describes resisting the reflex to say “I love you”.

As he points out, he nearly said “I love you”, but if he had it wouldn’t have meant anything. It would just have been a mechanical reaction.

And finally, it is possible for more than one account to apply. Here’s a clear case:

> **Cora Sandel, Alberte trilogy**
>
> Near the end of the final volume, Alberte’s cruel and dishonest partner Sivert reminds her that she once said, “Jeg elsker dig.”
>
> Alberte barely remembers saying it and reconstructs it as something that may simply have escaped her during an intimate moment.
>
> Sivert, however, insists that saying it was significant: “Du sa det. Og det er en feil. Det er mannen som først skal si denslags.”
>
> The case is important because the two characters construe the same utterance differently.
>
> For Alberte, the original utterance seems largely non-performative, perhaps reflexive or truth-conditional.
>
> Sivert retrospectively imposes a performative interpretation.
>
> The scene also appears integrally related to the novel’s feminist structure. Sivert has already announced that he intends to exploit the legal resources available to him in order to impose his will on Alberte. Here, he arguably does something parallel with language: he claims authority to decide what her earlier words must have meant and uses that interpretation against her.

And I’m indebted to Jessica for the following particularly fine example:

> **War and Peace**
>
> In *War and Peace*, wealthy, hapless Pierre is bewitched by the beautiful, idiotic Helen and her designing family, who conspire to see her married to Pierre (and his immense fortune). Pierre, in response to what seems to be a done deal, is confused about the situation and feels a sense of dread mingled with attraction, but tells himself that it is now definite and there is no point in asking whether it is good or bad. Casting around for something to say when it becomes clear that he is inevitably fated to marry a woman whose physical allure is her only attraction, they have a moment of total awkwardness in the place of intimacy.
>
> Pierre says “Je vous aime!”, having remembered what needed to be said on these occasions; but the words sound so meager that he feels ashamed of himself. A month and a half later he is married.
>
> Pierre recalls these words—expressed in the artificial, affected French of high society, rather than his soulful native Russian—several times throughout the novel, as representing the point at which he bound himself to a life of misery with a cruel, stupid woman.

Here, as Jessica points out, Pierre utters the words more out of politeness and convention than anything else, but they are simultaneously a binding promise.

I wish I could end with a neat resolution, explaining why this phrase has such unusual semantic properties. Despite having thought about it a fair amount, I can’t. But I hope that this will at least convince you that the question of what “I love you” means is less obvious than may first appear.
